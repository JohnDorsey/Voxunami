# Elliptical orbits, ships on exact rails, and long burns: a plan and an investigation (2026-11-01)

**A proposal, not a decision.** Written at the owner's request after the joint and f64 work (`decisions/joints-and-cars.md`, `history/joint-probe.md`). The owner asked for three things: (A) a plan for elliptical orbits, (B) whether ships can be kept on
128-bit fixed-point rails at all times, with Rapier used only for what only Rapier gives, and (C) how constant or varying thrust for a long time (ion drives, solar sails) could be integrated ahead of time, so that a preview is the
trajectory time warp would give. What is checked in the code is marked **[code]**; what comes from the web or memory is marked **[source]** or **[memory]**; what is my reasoning is marked **[mine]**.

## 0. Where things stand

- **(This section is how things stood when the plan was written, 2026-11-01; steps A0 to A3 below are done, so a rail can be an ellipse.)**
- **[code]** `MovingGrid::rail: Option<Rail>` exists for any grid and is saved in ship files; `put_ships_on_their_rails` sets the pose of every grid with a rail at the start of a tick (`world.rs`). Only planets get rails today (data packs); `Orbit` is a
  *circle* (`around`, `normal`, `radius`, `period`, `phase`), worked out in 128-bit fixed point (`exact_circle.rs`: CORDIC sine and cosine in 126 bits, `ExactOffset` = `i128` bricks plus a rest in steps of 2^-32 voxel). The velocity is plain `f64`. **(Since 2026-11-03 it is worked out by `wide_kepler`: see E8; `ExactOffset` is the same.)**
- **[code]** The offset is exact, but it is then added to the parent's `WorldPosition` (an `f32` offset in a brick), so the stored pose of a moon is rounded to about 2 micrometres at every tick. It is a function of the tick, so it does not accumulate. **(2026-11-02, later: the offset is an `f64` now, 4e-15 voxel at the most, and so are the speeds, the masses and the angles of a player: see "f64 for physics" in `design-decisions.md`.)**
- **[code]** A ship that is far from every player is frozen: its speed is zeroed (`set_grid_frozen`). Parked ships are in files and are not carried by a planet that moves. There is no conversion of a free ship to a rail or back. A ship's gravity is a sum of the pulls of every
  massive grid, in `f32` (`gravity_at`; `f64` since 2026-11-02); inside a planet's sphere it leaves out the pull of what the planet goes round (`gravity_in_the_free_fall_of`).
- **[code]** Frames: a `BodyWorld` never moves; a frame that "follows" a planet subtracts the planet's displacement since the frame was made (`Follow`), and the planet's speed from velocities, so what rests on the planet rests in the frame.

## A. Elliptical orbits

**Shape of the change [mine; revised 2026-11-01 after the owner's questions, see A2].** `Orbit` gains `ratio` = 1 - e (the periapsis distance over the semi-major axis; **not** the eccentricity, A2 says why) and `periapsis` (the angle of the nearest point in the orbit's plane, from the same reference direction the circle starts at).
`radius` becomes the semi-major axis a (for a circle it is the radius), `period` stays (given for a planet, worked out from the parent's mass and a for a ship), `phase` stays the mean anomaly at tick 0. With `ratio` 1.0 the result must be **the same bits as the circle today**: that is the first test.
**No epoch for ellipses (the owner asked whether higher precision when the orbit is made would do instead; it does, and I had the reason wrong).** I first proposed an epoch because the phase "at time 0" of a ship put on a rail at tick N looked like a huge angle (n x t) rounded to `f64`. It is not: `turn_fraction` already takes the turns gone since tick 0 modulo one *in whole numbers*,
so the phase at tick 0 of an orbit made at tick N is the phase at N less the turns gone by N, worked out exactly and rounded once, into [0, 2 pi): the precision an epoch would give. An epoch is for hyperbolas (no period; the time of periapsis is the natural number) and perhaps for ellipses very near a parabola (A2, point 2); decide then.

**Mathematics, all in whole numbers [mine, standard].**
1. Mean anomaly M = phase + time / period, as a fraction of a turn in 128 bits (the existing `turn_fraction`, exact however long the world has run).
2. Kepler's equation E - e sin E = M, written in turns (E - (e / 2 pi) sin(2 pi E) = M) and solved by Newton's method with a fixed number of steps (a count, never a time: `design-decisions.md`). **As built (step A2): ** a first guess in `f64`
   (the root of the cubic E^3/6 + (1 - e) E = M where e is 0.3 or more, M + e sin M below it, then eight Newton steps in `f64` written without the cancellation of E - e sin E near 0; `libm` and the machine's operations only), then **three** Newton steps in whole numbers, each a sine and cosine and a long division.
   There is **no bisection bracket**: the iterate is only kept in the half turn, where the equation is convex and increasing, so Newton's method is safe from either side, and the guess is good to 1e-15, which two steps take past 126 bits (A2, point 5 had this start keeping a pure fixed-point Newton to 6 to 8 steps; with the `f64` guess three, one to spare, **[measured]** in A2 below). Cost: **[measured]** 2.7 microseconds a solve (A2 below).
3. Position in the plane: x = a (cos E - e), y = a sqrt(1 - e^2) sin E, with the fixed-point square root that the plane code already has; turned by the periapsis angle and into the plane's basis (`exact_circle::plane`), multiplied in 256 bits as the circle is.
4. Speed: v = (a n / r) (-sin E, sqrt(1 - e^2) cos E), n = 2 pi / period, r = a (1 - e cos E), as `f64` from the same exact sine and cosine (as the circle does now).
5. Hyperbolas (e above 1, for flybys and escapes) use sinh and cosh and a different Kepler equation, M = e sinh H - H, with no period; **a second step**, after the ellipse is pinned. Parabolas are not made (`ratio` 0 is one; the same pair covers hyperbolas as `ratio` below 0).

**Conversions [mine].** State to elements (position and velocity against the parent, and the parent's mass parameter mu = G M, which the game has: planets declare a mass, `planet-spec.md`): the standard vectors h = r x v, the eccentricity vector, a from the energy. The degenerate cases (a circle has no periapsis; a flat orbit has no
plane) get a fixed rule (reference direction; the level's +y). Elements to state is the code above. A round trip must return the state to within what the elements' `f64` hold (about 1e-16 of the orbit's size).

## A2. How many bits the rails need (the owner's questions, with measurements, 2026-11-01)

The owner asked: is 128-bit fixed point enough for orbits across the universe; how are orbits of extreme eccentricity stored; does the apoapsis go to infinity before an orbit is hyperbolic; what would 256 bits cost. The reproductions are in `history/spikes/rails/`
(`kepler_conditioning.py`, `kepler_iterations.py`, `cordic_bench/`; they need `mpmath` for the references).

**1. Evaluating a rail is enough at every size the universe has.** **[code]** Positions are `i128` bricks (2^132 voxels, 5 x 10^39 m), an orbit's radius is limited to 2^120 voxels, the observable universe is 8.8 x 10^26 m (2^89.5); the test
`an_orbit_a_very_long_way_out_is_where_the_exact_turn_puts_it_to_a_small_fraction_of_a_voxel` (`engine/world_storage/tests/rail.rs`) checks offsets out to a radius of 2^90 against worked-out values to within 2^-31 voxel, and the sine and cosine are tested against 120 digits.
**[mine, arithmetic]** The rounding of the angle moves a body by a few x 2 pi R x 2^-126: a few 1e-10 voxel at R = 2^90 (about the size of the 2^-32 grid of the output, and the existing test's tolerance is 2^-31), a few 1e-7 at 2^100, a fraction of a voxel at the cap of 2^120.
A `WorldPosition` shows 2e-6 at best, so none of it is seen out to 2^100.
The longest period (2^80 seconds, `LONGEST_PERIOD`) and the time (an `i128`) are not limits either.

**2. Eccentric ellipses: the limit is the time near periapsis, not the size.** Rounding the mean anomaly to 2^-126 of a turn moves a body at periapsis by 2 pi x 2^-126 x sqrt(2) x q x (1 - e)^(-3/2) (q the periapsis distance): there the body is fast and the period is long.
**[measured]** (`kepler_conditioning.py`: the exact Kepler solution at M = 0 and one unit later, `mpmath` at 1200 bits) the formula agrees with the exact displacement to four digits where e is near 1. For q = 1e9 m, the metres one unit of M moves the body at periapsis:

| apoapsis / periapsis | 1 - e | at 126 bits | at 254 bits |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1e3 | 2e-3 | 1.2e-24 | 3.4e-63 |
| 1e6 | 2e-6 | 3.7e-20 | 1.1e-58 |
| 1e9 | 2e-9 | 1.2e-15 | 3.4e-54 |
| 1e12 | 2e-12 | 3.7e-11 | 1.1e-49 |
| 1e15 | 2e-15 | 1.2e-6 | 3.4e-45 |
| 1e18 | 2e-18 | 3.7e-2 | 1.1e-40 |
| 1e21 | 2e-21 | 1.2e3 | 3.4e-36 |

The largest apoapsis / periapsis for which that stays within the output grid (2^-32 voxel), from the formula: at 126 bits 3.4e16 for q = 1e3 m, 3.4e14 for 1e6 m, **3.4e12 for 1e9 m**, 3.4e10 for 1e12 m; at 254 bits 1.7e42, 1.7e40, 1.7e38, 1.7e36.
**[mine]** For a centre of one solar mass and the longest period (2^80 s), a = 1.7e22 m, so with q = 1e9 m the ratio is 3.4e13: about 7 nm at periapsis, invisible; a centre a million times heavier, about 7 micrometres. This is the least error any 126-bit design has (the rounding of M alone):
the solver (step A2) adds to it, **[measured]**: it is right to about 8 units of 2^-128 turn for each unit of 1 / (1 - e cos E) on the reference rows (12 on 1,260 more), which is the sine's own accuracy and no more; at a ratio of 1e-18 and a mean anomaly of one unit it cannot tell the body from periapsis (the true anomaly is 3e-21 of a turn).

**3. Storing the shape: not as the eccentricity.** **[mine, arithmetic]** An `f64` e is a multiple of 1.1e-16 just below 1. At a ratio of 1e12 (1 - e = 2e-12) it has four digits, so the periapsis a(1 - e) is uncertain by 5e-5 of itself (50 km at 1e9 m), and an ellipse of ratio 2e16 cannot be told from a parabola.
So the plan stores the semi-major axis a and `ratio` = 1 - e, both `f64`: a circle is `ratio` exactly 1.0; the periapsis a x ratio and the apoapsis a x (2 - ratio) are products, with no cancellation; `ratio` 0 is a parabola and below 0 a hyperbola, so the same pair serves step 2.
The alternative, periapsis and apoapsis distances, reads better but breaks at the parabola. Confidence: medium.

**4. Does the apoapsis go to infinity before the orbit is hyperbolic? Yes.** With the periapsis fixed, apoapsis a (2 - ratio) and the period (as a^1.5) both go to infinity as `ratio` goes to 0. The energy crosses zero smoothly (negative: ellipse; zero: parabola; positive: hyperbola), so there is no gap, only a stretch where the ellipses are very long.
Two limits stop it first here: the period is at most 2^80 s, and (patched conics) a ship leaves its parent's sphere of influence before an apoapsis beyond it, so only orbits round the root body (a comet round a star) can have an extreme ratio.

**5. The solver near a parabola.** **[measured]** (`kepler_iterations.py`: guarded Newton and Halley, `mpmath`) Steps to reach 2^-126, for 1 - e from 0.5 down to 1e-30 and M from 1e-30 to 3.1, worst case. From the usual start (M + e sin M) Newton needs 6 steps at 1 - e = 0.5 and **63 at 1e-30** (Halley 37);
from the root of the cubic E^3/6 + (1 - e) E = M it needs **6 to 7 at every e** (Halley 5); at 2^-254, 7 to 8 (Halley 6). So "a handful of steps" holds only with the cubic start. Each step is one sine and cosine, plus one for the answer.

**6. What wider costs.** **[measured]** (`cordic_bench`: the sine and cosine only, a release build; three steady runs, a fourth run disturbed by other work had the same ratios; the 126-bit limb version is bit-identical to the repo's on six angles, the 254-bit one is within 15 units of the last place of `mpmath` and the 190-bit one within 13):

| width | ns per sine and cosine | against today |
|---|---|---|
| 126 bits, `i128`, as `exact_circle.rs` | 450 to 570 | 1 |
| 126 bits, the same algorithm on 64-bit limbs | 690 to 810 | 1.5 |
| 190 bits | 1,560 to 1,760 | 3.1 to 3.5 |
| 254 bits | 2,700 to 2,900 | 5 to 6 |

**[measured at 126 bits, estimated at 254]** An ellipse is 4 sine-and-cosines (the first guess is made in `f64`, which saves the other four I counted) and three divisions: 2.7 microseconds at 126 bits (step A2; 3.4 with the setup of the `Ellipse`), where I had estimated 4 to 5; scaling by the measured 5 to 6 for the sine and cosine, about 15 at 254. The server makes 20 ticks a second (**[code]** `config.rs`), so 50 elliptical bodies cost about 3 ms in a second of one core at 126 bits and about 15 ms at 254.
A rail is a function of the tick, so a ship nobody observes needs no evaluation at all. **Not measured:** the rest of a 256-bit rail: a 256 x 256 to 512 product, a real multi-limb reduction in `turn_fraction` (its modulus is below 2^87 today), 256 steps of long division in `fraction_in_turns`,
references at 160 digits, a new rail format; I would guess one or two phases of work. The door stays open: the stored elements do not depend on the width the sine and cosine are worked in, so widening later changes bits below 1e-10 voxel of the results, not the files.

**7. Where I land.** Stay at 126 bits. It is right for every size of orbit the universe has, and for ellipses up to the ratios of point 2, which is more than any orbit inside a sphere of influence can have. What 254 bits buy is ratios above 1e12 to 1e16 at nanometre accuracy, which the period limit and the
spheres of influence rule out for anything inside a sphere of influence. The costs are 5 to 6 times on the hottest rail path and a rewrite of the arithmetic. Confidence: fairly high on points 1 to 4, medium on 6 and 7 (unbuilt parts).
The things that do need care are the ones above: `ratio` instead of the eccentricity, the cubic start, and a test at `1 - e` of 1e-18.

## B. Ships on exact rails, Rapier only where only Rapier helps

**The idea, in one sentence [mine].** The rail is the ship's **centre of mass**; Rapier works on the ship's **parts relative to the rail** in a frame that follows it, and on interactions that need contacts; the big, exact numbers never go through `f64`.

**What it needs, and what exists.**
1. *The rail as the authority.* **[code]** Planets already work so (a pose written from the rail each tick). For a ship the same: its pose is the rail's state at the tick, rounded for the world's record. The exact state (`ExactOffset`) must be kept next to the pose, not rebuilt from the rounded pose,
   or the exactness is lost at once (today the rounding happens at `translated_by_bricks`).
2. *Free flight without Rapier.* A ship that only coasts, or burns on a program, is a **kinematic body that is put where its rail says** (the planets' case, `BodyKind::Kinematic`); Rapier does not integrate its gravity at all. This removes the integrator error of `f32` forces
   (`future-goals.md`, "forces over solver steps") from free flight, observed or not, and makes observed and unobserved flight the same trajectory. **Believed [mine]**: the biggest single gain for the owner's goal. Needs: gravity of the model of section A's questions (one parent at a time) for rail and body alike.
3. *A frame that follows the ship's own rail*, for the motion of cars against each other (joints, a docking, a hinge). **[code]** `Follow` does this for planets: places and velocities of the frame are the world's less the carrier's. It would be generalised to follow any rail, but **with no area of effect**: the frame holds only
   the cars of that one ship (the owner does not want stations that carry other ships; see `future-goals.md`, "Tractor beam"). The cars' state in it is small (their offsets from the rail, in `f64`), so a round trip through `f64` loses about 1e-16 of those small numbers, not of a distance from the origin. **[mine]**: this is the "Encke" idea, the part that the rail
   gets exactly and Rapier only the small difference.
4. *The two-level rule.* The sum of the outside forces on all the cars, over the whole mass, is the rail's acceleration; the cars feel in their frame what they feel less that acceleration (so a car pushed by an engine and one that is not are pushed against each other, and the whole is not moved twice). **[mine]** Standard: the centre of mass moves as if
   all the outside forces acted on a point mass; the rest is relative motion. A gravity that differs across the ship (tides) is left out. **Felt acceleration** (`future-goals.md`) comes out of it for free: a player aboard feels the frame's acceleration.
5. *Contact with something outside* (a planet's ground, another ship). **What "a shared frame" means:** Rapier finds a contact only between bodies that are in the same Rapier world, in the same coordinates. A ship's own frame (point 3) holds only its own cars, so it can touch nothing outside.
   When a ship comes within reach of something it could touch, its cars are moved **into a frame that holds both**: near a planet, the planet's following frame, which exists today (**[code]** `Follow`: the planet's terrain is kinematic in it and the ships near the planet are dynamic bodies in it);
   for two ships, a frame that follows the heavier one's rail, in which both are dynamic and every number is some hundreds of metres, so `f64` is plenty. While there a ship is a dynamic body as it is now, and an impulse changes its rail: its elements are made again from the state it leaves with once it is clear.
   A ship that is in no contact is kinematic on its rail. It is not an area of effect: the frame is chosen by who is near what, and nothing is carried. **The part I am least sure of** is the rule for entering and leaving (a distance with a margin, so that a ship does not flip in and out; three ships in a pile): to design when B reaches it. The conversion cost is the `f64` rounding of small relative numbers, about 1e-16 (section "What reading a ship back ... costs" of `joint-probe.md` measures the other, larger, `f32` costs this avoids).
6. *Freeze and park become "go on rails".* The loss of speed (`set_grid_frozen`) is replaced by making the elements from the state; loading is the elements at the clock. A ship that coasted unobserved keeps **the same elements** (not made again from a state), so a teleport to it and away again cannot change its orbit. A ship that was pushed or thrusted while observed has new elements.
   Parked ships are then carried exactly by the clock (today a parked ship is left behind by a planet that moves on).
7. *Precision decision [mine].* A relative state in `f64` integrates a burn well enough (a ship 1e9 m from its parent is placed to 1e-7 m per step; a million steps of rounding are 1e-4 m at the worst). What needs 128 bits is the **absolute** place and the angles of the rails of the parent chain (an angle over 10^12 seconds), which are already so.
   So I would keep the burn integrator in `f64` (with `libm`, a fixed sequence of steps) and the rails in fixed point, **not** a 128-bit integrator. This is an estimate; the experiment is E1 below. The decision about the `f32` offset of `WorldPosition` waits for it, as the owner said.

### B1 as built: ships that coast (2026-11-02)

The first piece of B is the one that fixes the drift `games/voxunami_cave/tests/orbit.rs` measured (a circle gains 3.5e-4 of its energy in a tenth of a lap, because the pull is held for the whole tick): **a free ship that nothing disturbs goes on a rail, and a ship on one comes off it when anything happens to it.** It is `engine/server/src/server_world/coasting.rs`; its rules are tested without bodies in `server_world/tests/coasting_ships.rs`, and with them in `orbit.rs`.

- **The rail is an orbit round a planet, from the state.** After [`QUIET_SECONDS`] (1 s) of being left alone, a ship's place (its **centre of mass**) and speed against the planet that pulls it most (mass over distance squared, among the planets with a lower number than the ship's that are held or on a rail and weigh what pulls) are made into an orbit with `Orbit::from_place` at the time of the clock. `put_ships_on_their_rails` places the centre of mass and puts the origin of the ship back from it by the turn of the ship (a planet's centre of mass is its origin, so nothing moves for planets).
- **Where it differs from B as written above, and why.** (1) *A disturbance takes the ship off its rail where it is, and it is made again from the state once it is left alone; nothing is "exact rail plus the change of speed that the physics gave".* That was written when the state was an `f32`, and the rail's exactness was to carry what the physics could not; with positions and speeds in `f64` (4e-15 voxel) the state after a push is as good as the exact offset (2^-32 voxel) was, and the whole two-level rule of B4 is not needed for a ship that is either on a rail or a body of the solver's. It is still the way for joints and cars (B3, B4), which are not built. (2) *The bodies hold a ship on a rail: it is a fixed body* (`BodyKind::Fixed`, as a held ship is), so Rapier neither moves it nor gives it gravity, and the n-body pull in a rail ship's frame is the planet's alone (patched conics, question 1).
- **What keeps a ship off a rail, or takes it off:** held; turning at all (a rail holds a place, not a turn; **a tumbling ship is a body of the solver's, with its old error**: a steady spin about a principal axis could be a `Spin`, not done); a level with a pull of its own; a force, a torque, a docking, a move or a speed put on it by anyone but the bodies (`ServerWorld::disturb`; the bodies write with `move_grid_by_bodies`); another ship or planet nearer than a margin of 64 voxels and a second of the speed it closes at (twice that to go on a rail, once to stay, so that a ship does not go on and off); the sphere of a planet on a rail that carries what is near it (a quarter more to go on); the sphere of influence (`a (m/M)^(2/5)`) of a planet that is not what it goes round. An orbit is refused if it comes lower than the **extent** of the planet's ground (`extent_bricks`, from the middle) and the margin, or goes out past the sphere of influence of the planet.
- **The extent is the reach of the ground**, as `pack-format.md` says (the radius and the hills "with a brick or two to spare"), but Cave's planet has 2,100 bricks (67,200 voxels) for a ball of 63,710 and hills of 160: a ship in a circle 2 km over the hills is **inside the extent and stays a body of the solver's**. `orbit.rs` has both: that circle (the old error, kept as a test of it) and one at 70,000 voxels, clear of it, which is on a rail after a second and holds its energy and angular momentum to 1e-12 (the criterion of the review was 1e-5, and the solver's was 3e-4).
- **Frozen and parked.** A frozen ship that is on a rail keeps its speed (`set_grid_frozen` zeroed it; it does not for a ship on a rail, which has no speed of its own to lose); a ship that is saved on a rail and brought back is where the clock says (the rail is in its file), so **a ship that coasts unobserved goes on round, and a parked one does too**. A frozen ship that is not on a rail (turning, below the extent, near something) still loses its speed, as before.
- **Not done, and where it will bite.** (a) Entering and leaving a shared frame near a planet's ground is still the old frames; B5 above is not touched, and the margin and the extent are the whole rule. (b) A planet's gravity as the bodies feel it near the ground is softened (`G m r / (r^2 + 1)^(3/2)`); a rail is not; they agree to (1/r)^2 beyond the extent. (c) The first second of a ship's life as a free body is still the solver's, so the elements carry the error of one second of it (5.8e-6 of the energy in the Cave test), once, and not again. (d) A ship on a rail is a fixed body, so `keep_terrain` asks for none of a planet's ground for it (it never did for a fixed ship); it could never be near any, outside the extent. **Verified by a mutation (2026-11-02) to be untested at the level of the world**: with the rule taken out the Cave's tests still pass, because a ship on a rail is put back where the clock says whatever the solver does to it; the rule (`grid_bodies::body_kind`) has a unit test and no test that sees it in a world, and what it saves is the solver's time for such a ship, which no test measures. (e) The apsis-timed answer to a change of the planet's mass (the owner's rule, 2026-11-02) is the next piece (B2).

### B2 as built: a ship answers a change of the mass at an end of its ellipse (2026-11-02)

The owner's rule (`future-goals.md`, "A planet whose mass changes"): a ship answers a change of the mass of the planet it goes round at the apsis where the answer is safe: an **increase at the periapsis**, a **decrease at the apoapsis**, and at the other end it waits. Built in `coasting.rs` (`answer_a_change_of_mass`) on two additions to `world_storage::rail`: `Orbit::mass_parameter` (`n^2 a^3`: the elements say the mass they were made with, so **no history of the planet's mass is kept**: a ship compares the elements with the planet as it is, and so a ship that was parked or unobserved while the planet changed answers at the next end it passes) and `Orbit::apsides_passed(from, to)` (which ends the body passes in a stretch of time, from the mean anomaly in whole numbers of turns; a circle has none, and answers at once). A ship answers in the tick whose half tick either side the end is in, so it is at the nearest tick to the end.

- **What it does at the end:** the same fit as going on a rail, from the place and the speed of the moment (`Orbit::from_place` with the planet's mass parameter now), then the same checks (above the ground and what the planet carries, inside the planet's sphere of influence). A ship that fails them, or is on no ellipse (the planet pulls nothing now, or the speed is above that of escape), is taken off its rail as it is and goes on as the bodies have it.
- **Counts as a change** a mass parameter that differs from the elements' by more than a part in a billion; so does a change of the game's pull strength.
- **What the measurement found about the rule (not in the rule as written).** An end keeps its distance, but **as an end, not as the same end**: at the nearest point the speed is that of a circle for (1 + e) times the mass, so an increase of more than that factor makes the point the *far* end of the new ellipse and the near end goes *down* by what is left (e = 1/3, a mass half as much again: the near end 5,000 goes to 4,000, a fifth, where a fifth more mass moves it by under the half percent the test allows); likewise a decrease below (1 - e) of the mass turns the far end into the near end and throws the far end out (a half of the mass: 10,000 goes to 20,000). The nearest point is still the best place for an increase, but for a big change the rule does not keep a ship out of the ground; the check of the orbit does (a ship the new orbit would put in the ground is taken off its rail, and falls). **The owner accepted it as it is (2026-11-02):** the answer may move the apses, a change that threatens a ship would have to be very large, and it is to be revisited on the feedback of players if need be. Tests: `server_world/tests/coasting_ships.rs` (`a_change_that_is_more_than_the_eccentricity...`).
- **Not done:** nothing in the game changes the mass of a planet yet (a planet's mass is the file's), so this is built and tested but not seen in play; the history of masses for a *planet* that a ship has to catch up with over many passes is not needed (one answer to the mass now, at the next end).
- **The error of the nearest tick.** A ship is at most half a tick from the end when it answers (50 ms at 8 km/s is 400 m): the new orbit's end is that much off the old one's, second order in the distance; the tests allow 0.5 percent of the radius and hold.

### E1 done: fine control of an orbit over huge distances (2026-11-02)

**The owner's requirement (2026-11-02).** In an orbit round some body with the periapsis in kilometres and the apoapsis in light years, a player at the periapsis throttles a thruster far below 1 percent and changes the apoapsis by amounts small enough to tune a rendezvous with another ship. Which parts of the game are too coarse for it? The spike is `history/spikes/rails/e1_fine_control_test.rs` (the numbers below; the positions are checked by `e1_check_positions.py` against the 120-digit reference). The orbit: round a body of the Earth's `G M` (4e14), nearest 7,000 km, farthest one light year (9.46e15 m): a semi-major axis of 4.73e15 m, a ratio (1 - e) of 1.48e-9 and a period of 1.02e17 s (3.2 thousand million years, 2e18 ticks). Marked **[measured]** (a real program, this code) or **[read]** (from reading the code, not run).

**What is accurate enough.**
- **[measured] The rail and the Kepler solver.** Places on this orbit, at the nearest point, a tick on, 12,000 ticks on, a hundred thousand million ticks on, at the far end and past a whole period, agree with the 120-digit reference to 1.2e-10 m at the worst (the step of the fixed point is 2.3e-10 m). Nothing is lost at a ratio of 1.5e-9 or at a light year.
- **[measured] The spacing of the elements.** The axis has a spacing of 1 m (so the apoapsis moves in steps of 2 m), the nearest distance 1e-9 m, the period 16 s; the mass parameter the elements say (`n^2 a^3`) is 1.1e-16 from the one they were made with. A rendezvous at the far end is not limited by the period's 16 s: a body there goes at 7.9e-6 m/s, so 16 s is 0.1 mm. (At the nearest point it goes at 10.7 km/s, so 16 s there is 170 km: a rendezvous at the nearest point on an orbit this long is not fine in time, and two ships on the same elements would have to be on the same ones to the bit.)
- **[read] Positions and times.** A position is an `i128` brick and an `f64` offset (4e-15 voxel) and the rail's time an `i128` of ticks; a light year is 3e14 bricks and 2e18 ticks is a tenth of what a `u64` clock holds.

**What is not accurate enough, in the order a push meets it.**
1. **[read] There is no throttle.** A pilot's keys are -1, 0 or +1 (`PilotKeys`, `MoveInput`: three `i8` and flags) and a fixed `THRUST` of 24 m/s^2 (twice that with sprint), and `pilot_force` also holds the ship up against gravity and drags its speed (`DRAG` of 1 a second): it is a hover model, not a thruster. The least push the keys can make is one tick, 1.2 m/s: twelve hundred times the 1 mm/s that already moves the apoapsis of the orbit below by 253 light years (and an orbit with a speed that much higher at the nearest point is not bound). **Needs:** an analog throttle on the wire (an `f64`; `f64` has the range: a throttle of 1e-19 is a number) and a thrust that is a ship's engines and not a hover assist.
2. **[measured] A push cannot be kept in a speed that is an `f64`.** At 10.7 km/s the spacing of a speed is 1.8e-12 m/s, and a push of that size at the nearest point moves the apoapsis by **4.35e9 m**; one spacing of the axis (1 m) is 8.4e-22 m/s. So the apoapsis cannot be tuned to better than 4 million kilometres by anything that adds the push to a velocity, and a push smaller than 1.8e-11 m/s^2 a tick (7.6e-13 of the game's 24) is lost entirely. A table of the apoapsis made from a state after a push (ideal against realised): from 1e-7 to 1e-10 m/s the realised change is within 3 percent of the ideal (1e-6 is 34 percent off: the orbit is nearly a parabola and a first-order ideal is not); at 1e-11 it is 10 percent off, at 1e-12 about a tenth of the ideal, and from 1e-13 down it is the noise of the fit (2.6e8 m), whatever the push. (Pushes of 1e-3 to 1e-5 m/s make the orbit unbound: at the nearest point 1 mm/s moves the apoapsis by 253 light years.)
3. **[measured] Making an orbit again from a state loses the apoapsis at the nearest point.** `Orbit::from_state` on the exact state: at the nearest point the apoapsis is **2.6e8 to 1.2e10 m** out (2.7e-8 to 1.2e-6 of it) and the period 4e9 to 1.9e11 s; 450 m out at 1e13 m from the centre, 0 at the far end. It grows with the length of the ellipse, as a part of the apoapsis: 1e-15 at an apoapsis of 1e7 m, 5e-15 at 1e9, 8e-13 at 1e10, 5e-11 at 1e12, 8e-9 at 1e14, 5.8e-7 at 1e16 and 1e-4 at 1e18 (the energy is the difference of two terms of 2 / r, a thousand million times what is left, and the state has no more digits: it is the information, not the working). So **every disturbance that makes an orbit again from a state (B1: every push; B2: the answer to an increase of the mass, at the nearest point) costs this**, and it cannot be done better from an `f64` state at the nearest point.
4. **[measured] The solver cannot integrate a burn that is to be this fine.** Rapier's integration of a ship at orbital speed gains 3.5e-4 of its energy in a tenth of a lap (`orbit.rs`). A fixed-step leapfrog in `f64` (the better case), against the exact rail on an ellipse of 7,000 to 14,000 km, steps of a tick: second order is 6e-10 of the energy out and the place 0.34 m out after 4.7 laps (1e6 steps), 3.5 m after 47 laps (1e7 steps); a fourth-order composition of it (Yoshida) is 1e-13 out in energy and 5e-5 m out after 4.7 laps, 2.4e-3 m after 47 laps (roundoff takes over: the error grew by 50 for 10 times the steps), and with steps of half a second 3.4e-4 m after 47 laps. An orbit this long would need a hundred thousand million times the steps (a fixed step is the same near the nearest point and far out; a thin ellipse of 7,000 km to 7e6 km, two laps at 10 ticks a step, is 2.6e8 steps and 44,000 km out at second order, 71 m out at fourth). The energy error of 1e-13 is a part in 1e13; the tuning above is a part in 1e19. **No integrator in `f64` in Cartesian coordinates reaches it, however high its order.**
5. **[read] The sphere of influence.** A rail round a planet that itself goes round something is refused if its farthest point is past the planet's sphere (`PastTheSphere`); a light year is some 10^6 times the sphere of any planet. So such an orbit exists only round a body that goes round nothing, or when the rule gives way; what a ship does when its conic leaves a sphere (a hand-over to the body that pulls it then) is not built (hyperbolas are the plan's next step after ellipses, A, and the hand-over is a part of it).

**What this says about the plan (B7 and C).** B7 said "a relative state in `f64` integrates a burn well enough" and kept the burn integrator in `f64`: **that is right for a burn that is to be good to a metre or a part in 10^9, and wrong for this requirement.** The way that meets it is the one C already names as probably best, made stricter:
- **A burn changes the elements, not a state.** The push is put into Gauss's equations for the orbit (the axis changes by `2 a^2 (v . f) / mu` per second for an acceleration `f`, the nearest distance, the plane and the direction of the nearest point by their own), computed from the rail's own state and the small acceleration, so the number that is added is as exact as the push (a relative 1e-16 of itself) and nothing is the difference of two large ones. For a push along the speed this is exact: the change of energy is the work, `f . v`.
- **The sum is kept in more digits than an `f64`.** The axis moves by less than its spacing each tick for a small throttle; the sum of those moves has to be kept in a double-double (two `f64`) or a 128-bit fixed number and put into the elements when it is more than their spacing, or the elements themselves carry the extra digits (`radius` and `period` as a pair, or the energy as a fixed-point number). A 1 m spacing of the axis is enough for 1 km of apoapsis; for a rendezvous to the metre the pair is wanted. **To decide** (the owner): the finest tuning of the apoapsis that is to be promised (1 m is what an `f64` axis gives with no change).
- **A burn is on a rail, not in the solver.** A ship under thrust far from anything it could touch stays kinematic and its elements are changed each tick; Rapier is for contacts (B5). This is B1 made to include thrust: a force from a pilot or a program no longer takes the ship off its rail if nothing is near, it changes the rail.
- **A push that is a collision or a bump** (an impulse of `dv` from contact) is the same: the change of energy is `v . dv + dv^2 / 2` from the rail's exact state and the small `dv`, never a state made into an orbit again.
- The state is still right for what a state is right for (a ship that tumbles, one in contact), and for orbits whose ends are within 1e6 of each other (the fit loses under a part in 10^10 of the apoapsis up to an apoapsis of 1e12 m with a nearest point of 7e6 m).
**What each costs and what is not decided:** the throttle (item 1) is a game change (Cave); items 2 to 4 are all answered by the elements-and-accumulator design (step C, with a throttle that is a program: a start, an end and a thrust), which is a rewrite of what B1 does when a ship is pushed; item 5 is a decision about what a ship does when its orbit goes out of a sphere. None of it was built in this step.

### The fine-tuning promise and burns with any thrust (2026-11-02; analysis, nothing built)

**The promise** is in `future-goals.md`: planet to planet across a galaxy, up to ten gravity assists, the periapsis of the orbit lines reached exactly, and controlled to 1 mm; and a pilot loses no freedom (any spin, RCS, off-centre engines, at any time).

**Gauss's equations are for any force, not only one along the motion.** They give the rate of change of every element for a perturbing acceleration resolved in radial, transverse and normal parts (R, T, N); the aligned push is the case T only. What the orbit needs is the **net external force on the centre of mass** in the world's axes; a torque turns the ship and does not move its orbit, so an off-centre engine gives its force to the orbit and its torque to the attitude, and a pair of RCS thrusters gives the attitude a torque and the orbit nothing but their imbalance (which is a force, and is counted). The attitude stays the solver's (or a rigid-body integrator's) as it is; this is the two-level rule of B4. Under a spin, the push in a tick is the integral of the turning thrust over the tick (the attitude is known inside the tick), put in as one impulse at the middle of it: exact for a ship moving freely, off by the pull's curvature times the tick cubed.
**The form to use is the impulse form, in vectors**, not the angle elements: the specific energy `e`, the angular momentum vector `h` and the eccentricity vector `k` change for a velocity change `dv` at the state `(r, v)` by `de = v . dv + |dv|^2 / 2`, `dh = r x dv`, `dk = (dv x h + v x dh + dv x dh) / mu`: every term is a small number times a large one, so the change is as exact as the push, and nothing is singular for a circle or a flat orbit. The elements are the master and the state is read from them; **no element is made from a state that was rounded to `f64` first.**
**What a pilot notices:** nothing, so long as the ship is not in contact with anything. In contact (a landing, a docking, a ship near another) the ship is a body of the solver's in a shared frame as now, and the elements are made again from its state when it is clear. Thrust under time warp becomes possible, which Kerbal Space Program does not allow.

**Digits (estimated by `history/spikes/rails/flyby_amplification.py`, planar, patched conics, first order; [mine], not a measurement of the game).** A flyby magnifies an aim error `db` into `L x 2 mu db / (b^2 v^2)` at the next body, `L` being the leg: x1e4 for an Earth assist with a leg of 1.5e11 m, x1e3 to 2.4e4 for the giants, x1e5 to 1e7 for a star with a leg of 1e16 to 1e18 m. Six assists in a system multiply to 2e23, three stellar ones to 2e18, ten stellar ones to some 1e61 (extrapolated). For 1 mm at the end, with nothing corrected on the way, the numbers of the first leg need **131 bits** (six assists), **283 bits** (ten stellar); with a correction before the last assist only the last leg's x1.3e7 counts and a position needs a step of 7e-11 m, **which is about the 2.3e-10 m (2^-32) that a rail has now**. So today's rails are short of the promise by a few orders at the last leg and by many for the whole chain unless the pilot corrects on the way, which a pilot does.
**What the promise needs:** (1) the master chain (elements, states, the Kepler solver, the hyperbola, the sphere patching) in 256-bit arithmetic with a position step of about 2^-96 m, deterministic, no `f64` in it; (2) hyperbolas and the hand-over between spheres of influence done exactly (the crossing time is a root, found in the wide arithmetic); (3) a model for space between stars that is analytic (stars on rails that are functions of time; between spheres a straight line in the galaxy's frame, or any other closed form), because the digits come from arithmetic and not from stepping; (4) a flyby is not put in a solver frame by the sphere a planet carries what is near it with (1.5 times the reach of the ground): only a ship that can touch something leaves its rail (the unsolved rule of B5); (5) the throttle an `f64` and every push added exactly, so that a last trim of a part in 10^20 of the first burn is possible; (6) the orbit lines and the ship use one code and one set of inputs, so that a prediction is the execution to the bit.
**What it does not need:** a pilot who flies with perfect aim. The promise is a closed loop: the orbit lines are exact, the pilot trims, and the trims can be as small as they like. An open-loop single burn cannot land 1 mm in 10^21, and nothing could.

### E4 done: wide numbers, adaptive precision and the libraries (2026-11-02)

**What the owner asked (2026-11-02).** (1) Run the E4 spike: what a wide Kepler solver and a wide orbit cost. (2) Investigate **adaptive precision**: the game notices that the precision it has is not enough to reach sub-millimetre accuracy at the periapsis of the destination, and raises it; the destination is set by how far the player has chosen to predict, with a button to continue the prediction at each new sphere of influence (pressed 12 times: only the next 12 segments are checked). (3) What it would take to go to the logical extreme: 64 or 128 bits round a planet whose orbit stays in its sphere, 512 bits or more across the universe, assists off black holes, galaxies, planets and moons, chained dozens or hundreds of times; and how far libraries go.
The spike is `history/spikes/rails/e4/` (a Rust program with a workspace of its own, and `e4_chain.py`, which makes the chains and is the truth they are compared with), `e4_baseline_test.rs` (the rail we have now) and `e4_check_functions.py`. Marked **[measured]** (run here, Windows, and Linux in WSL where said), **[read]** (from reading code or documentation) or **[mine]** (an opinion or an estimate).

**The model measured.** The one the owner chose for now: patched conics, with a straight line between the spheres of influence. Bodies (stars, heavy stars, black holes of four million suns) move in straight lines at a constant speed (a proper motion) and pull inside their own sphere only. Inside a sphere the flyby is closed form: a hyperbola is symmetric about its line of apsides, so the ship leaves where the mirror image of the place it went in is, with the mirror velocity; the time inside is twice the time from the periapsis to the sphere (hyperbolic Kepler equation). Each flyby is aimed from the path the ship really takes (periapsis 5e8 to 1e12 m, legs 1e16 to 1e18 m), positions are exact multiples of 2^-10 m, speeds and masses are doubles, so the only difference between two runs is the rounding of the working. Tolerance: 0.1 mm (a tenth of the promise) on the periapsis of the last flyby. **A chain with no correction between the assists** (open loop) is the hard case; a pilot who corrects at every lens is the closed loop.

About **22 to 28 bits per assist** (a magnification of 2^22 to 2^28 per flyby, for these legs and periapses), plus about 100 bits for the distances (the universe over 0.1 mm is only 93 bits: **distance costs little, assists cost much**). Seeds 2 to 6 for 12, 24 and 48 assists agree to within one step of the ladder (a step is a factor of 1.25). So 512 bits is good for 18 to 22 assists, 1,024 for about 40 to 46, 2,048 for about 90, 4,096 for about 180, 8,192 for about 290. The first-order estimate of the previous section (283 bits for ten stellar assists) agrees with this to about 10 percent.
**Closed loop [measured].** One leg alone, from the exact state at the exit of the one before (state to 2^-30 m), needs **64 to 80 bits, 72 to 80 typically**, for 0.1 mm (192 legs; the ladder went 64, 72, 80, 88). So **128 bits, in a state held to 128 bits, is enough for any leg with about 48 bits to spare, and 64 bits (an `f64` has 53) is not** (at 64 bits the error of a leg was 1e-3 to 7e-2 m in the three legs looked at). Round a planet, in one sphere, 128 is plenty. Pilots correct, so the chain of a real flight is a series of closed legs; the long open chains are for the orbit lines (the preview), and for the player who wants to see "if I do nothing more".

**2. Detecting that the precision is not enough [measured].** The detector: run at `p` bits and at `2p`; if every periapsis of the horizon agrees to the tolerance, `p` is enough; if not, or if the run breaks (misses a sphere), double `p`. Over 8 chains and about 100 precisions it said "enough" **where it was not: never; "not enough" where it was: never**; it settled on the same precision as the truth (the least rung that stays under the tolerance). `adaptive` mode, a chain of 96, the button pressed K times: K 1 to 6 settles on 192 bits (1 attempt), 8 to 12 on 384, 16 to 32 on 768, 48 to 64 on 1,536, 96 on 3,072 (5 attempts); **the whole search, failed attempts and the runs at twice the bits included, costs 3 to 6 times the one run at the final precision**; the error against the truth was under 4e-12 m in every case. This is an observation on these chains, not a proof: a run and a run at twice the bits could in principle agree and both be wrong (a decision that flips the same way at both). **Where it is wrong, measured afterwards (`e4/tie_demo.py`, 2026-11-02): at a tie.** One flyby that grazes the sphere of influence: a ship goes along +x at 1e4 m/s and passes a lens at the origin at a distance of exactly 3e14 m, and the sphere is 3e14 m. With the sphere exactly that, the discriminant is exactly 0 at every precision and the ship "misses" at 192, 384 ... 6,144 bits; with a margin of 2^-300 (a sphere of 3e14 + 2^-300) 192 bits misses and 384 goes in (the attempt at 192, against 384, disagrees; 384 against 768 agrees: right); with a margin of 2^-1000 the attempts at 192 (against 384) and at 384 (against 768) have **both runs miss, and agree (wrong)**, the attempt at 768 disagrees with 1,536, and 1,536 agrees with 3,072. So (1) a loop that counts "both runs failed" or "both took the same branch" as agreement accepts a wrong answer at 192 bits, and `do_adaptive` does not (a failed run is a disagreement); (2) that rule never ends on an exact tie, so the loop needs a **ceiling** on the precision and a **rule for a tie at the ceiling** (grazing is a miss, say), the same on every machine; (3) the cost of a near tie is the number of bits of its margin. Balls give the first for free (a ball for the discriminant that holds zero says "cannot decide") and neither of the others. **A cheaper check (`e4/detector_margin.py`, measured 2026-11-02):** the check run need not be at twice the bits: the error of a run at `p + 64` is 2^-64 of that of the run at `p`. Checking against `p + 64` settled on the same precision as checking against `2p` for every horizon of the chain of 96 (K = 6, 12, 24, 48, 96: 192, 384, 768, 1,536, 3,072 bits) with the same errors against the truth, and the whole search cost **1.5 to 3.7 times the one run** at the settled precision (mostly 2 to 3) instead of 2.0 to 6.0 (mostly 3 to 5) with the check at twice the bits: three timings of each, Python and mpmath, and the times are noisy (short runs), while the precision it settled on and the errors were the same every time. So it saves about a third to a half of the search.
**Rigorous bounds [measured; mpmath `iv` and `puremp::Ball`, which agree: radius 6.7e-21 / 6.8e-21 m for 12 assists at 432 bits, 3.55e-7 / 3.5e-7 m for 48 at 1,376].** Interval or ball arithmetic is certain, and costs more bits: the range is under the tolerance at **432 bits for 12 assists (336 for the ordinary run), 864 for 24 (688), 1,376 for 48 (1,088), 3,424 for 96 (2,176), 6,400 or fewer for 192**: 1.3 to 1.6 times the bits (the dependency problem: the ranges of numbers that depend on each other are treated as independent), and the range is far wider than the real error (1e-21 against 1e-47 m). At the bits where the ordinary run is right, the ball cannot yet decide whether the ship goes into the next sphere (a "cannot decide" at 1,088 bits for 48 assists and 2,176 for 96). A rigorous chain of 96 assists at 3,424 bits takes 0.2 to 0.3 s, 192 at 6,400 bits 1.1 s (`puremp`, Windows). **The same chain of 12 assists, ball against ordinary run [measured, `e4_chain.py interval` and `ladder`, 2026-11-02]:** at 256 bits the ball "cannot say whether the ship enters the sphere" (the ordinary run is 1.3e7 m wrong, and the run at twice the bits differs from it by 1.3e7 m: the detector sees it); at 336 bits the ordinary run is 1.2e-17 m off and the ball is 3.6e8 m wide; at 432 bits 7.6e-47 m against 6.8e-21 m; at 544 bits 3.9e-80 against 1.3e-54; at 688 bits 1e-124 against 5.8e-98. So the ball is a **guaranteed** bound about 2^87 wider than the real error here, and it shrinks as fast as the error does; that gap is what the 1.3 to 1.6 times the bits pay for. In the same arithmetic `x - x` of a ball is not zero but twice its radius (a 53-bit ball of 0.1: plus or minus 1.4e-17), and ten rounds of `z = 2z - z` widen a ball of 1.4e-17 to 8e-13 though `z` has not changed: the dependency problem. The detector's number (the difference between the run and the run at twice the bits) equals the real error of the run to every digit printed in that table (1.274e7 against 1.274e7, 1.180e-17 against 1.180e-17).

For `+ - x /` on 300 pairs at each width, malachite, astro-float and puremp are right every time against whole-number arithmetic (`binary` mode); only dashu's subtraction is not. **4. What it would take [mine unless said].**
*Two kinds of precision.* **Storage**: what a ship's elements and a rail's elements are held in, fixed per kind of thing (an `f64` for a planet's pack data, 192 or 256 bits for a ship), the exact input of everything. **Evaluation**: how many bits a computation uses; chosen at run time. Storage never needs to grow: the future of an exactly stored state is a function of it, and a prediction needs more bits only to *evaluate* that function (the measured 22 to 28 bits per assist are evaluation bits). This is what makes adaptive precision possible at all.
*The rail we have, widened.* `ExactOffset` is `i128` bricks and a rest of 2^-32 m (2.3e-10 m anywhere in 5e39 m). The last leg of a closed loop needs a step of about 7e-11 m (section above), so the rest should be **2^-64 m (5e-20 m)**: a position of 192 bits, and the Kepler solver and the circle in 192 or 256 bits. [mine] A custom fixed-point solver at 256 bits would cost about 4 times the 126-bit one (CORDIC is quadratic in the width): about 15 to 20 us; malachite's solve at 256 bits is 25 us. I did not build it; by these numbers there is no reason to unless the licence rules malachite out.
*Lazy rails.* The ship holds its **last committed state** (the state at its last impulse, a collision or a hand-over it was changed at) and nothing else; where it is at a time is a function of that state, evaluated on demand to the bits the question needs and cached as a ball (a midpoint and a radius) at each hand-over; a question that wants a smaller radius than the cache has recomputes from the last entry that is good enough. Rounding then happens only at **commits** (a pilot's burn, a collision), which are the pilot's own acts, so the "jitter" of the map from control to outcome is the commit precision, 2^-192 or less, far under what 1 mm over 10^21 m needs. The alternative, rounding the state to a fixed width at every hand-over, makes the model depend on the width (the jitter then limits how far ahead a control can be tuned): possible, simpler to store, worse for the promise.
*The button.* K presses = a horizon of K segments. The preview runs the doubling loop above for those K segments only (seconds in the background, cancellable, cached by (state, K)); the K segments before the horizon are drawn from the cache; beyond it nothing is computed. Pressing again costs, at worst, one more run at the precision the longer horizon needs, and the doubling means the whole search is about 3 to 6 times the final run. The preview is display only; **the server decides** what really happens, with the same code, and for anything that changes the state (a hand-over, a commit) it must be certain, which for me means the ball arithmetic or the detector with a margin, and a rule for a tie (a decision whose ball holds zero at the largest precision allowed is decided in a fixed way).
*Determinism.* Results that are correctly rounded do not depend on the library, the machine or the order the precisions were tried (measured above), so the loop's schedule can be anything that is a function of the inputs (never of the clock; a time limit may stop the **preview**, never a decision of the server). The wire and the saves carry exact numbers (`i128` and mantissas with an exponent: a length-prefixed integer exists for coordinates above 64 bits); a new version of `engine:rail`.
*Cost of going on.* At 22 to 28 bits per assist and a flyby that costs about two transcendentals at the width: malachite does 300 assists (8.4 thousand bits) in 0.7 s; [mine, by the scaling of the speed table] 1,000 assists (25 thousand bits) in about 10 s, 4,000 (100 thousand bits) in about 6 minutes, 10,000 (250 thousand bits) in about half an hour: the libraries do not run out before 8 million bits, **time does**; a limit on the horizon is a rule of the game, not a limit of the numbers.
*Where it is hard.* (a) **Decisions**: which sphere is entered first, whether an orbit is bound, whether the ship hits the ground, which tick a crossing falls in: each needs the sign of a number, which needs more bits until the ball excludes zero (measured: a ball cannot decide at the bits where the ordinary run is right). (b) **The model is discontinuous** at a sphere boundary: a ship that grazes it goes in or not, and the periapsis jumps; no precision removes that; a pilot who aims for 1 mm must not graze. (c) **More conics**: hyperbola exit is closed form (measured); parabolas, near-parabolas, radial orbits and the elliptical Kepler equation (measured: 12 us to 18 ms from 128 to 16,384 bits) are needed; a moving parent that is itself on an ellipse needs its state at the hand-over time (a Kepler solve at the width) at each hand-over. (d) **The dependency problem** of rigorous arithmetic (1.3 to 1.6 times the bits) and the cost of writing every function for balls. (e) **Data**: stars and galaxies on rails that are functions of time, from catalogues, is a project of its own. (f) **Future galactic gravity** (the owner wants the door left open): an integrated arc has no closed form, so the interface should be "state at time `t` to a stated tolerance" (a ball), which a conic, a straight line and a numerical integrator in wide arithmetic can all answer; the integrator is the expensive one (a Taylor-series method with step control at 200+ bits, tens of microseconds a step) and would be a cached background computation, never per tick.
*Effort, in order of what unblocks what [mine, very rough]:* the wide `ExactOffset` and a 192/256-bit solver (small to medium); hyperbolas and the hand-over (medium); the evaluation layer (a trait over the library, balls, the doubling loop, conformance tests in CI on both systems) (medium to large); the lazy rail and its cache (medium); wire and file versions (small); the burn programs of step C on top (medium); the data pipeline (large, separate).
**Decisions this leaves to the owner:** (1) the **licence**: malachite is the fastest and LGPL-3.0-only (statically linked in a Rust binary that is a question for the project's licence; no `LICENSE` file or `license` field exists yet); astro-float and puremp are MIT and 2 to 10 times slower; the layer should hide the library either way, and the conformance tests make a swap safe; (2) commit semantics (lazy exact rails, which I recommend, against rounding at each hand-over); (3) the 2^-64 m step; (4) a limit on the horizon (a thousand assists?); (5) whether to build the evaluation layer next (it needs no change to the game, and everything else rests on it). **Answered by the owner (2026-11-02):** (1) LGPL libraries are acceptable, so `malachite` is open; the repository will be GPL or AGPL (undecided; no `LICENSE` file yet; both are, as far as I know, compatible with an LGPL-3.0 library, [mine, not legal advice], and AGPL's network clause is the one that reaches `serve cave`, a server others join); (2) the owner would keep 4,096-bit numbers in the world rather than lose a bit of a planned path ("so be it"), which removes the cost argument against a wide commit without choosing between lazy rails and a rounded commit. (5) **answered: build the evaluation layer next** (E6), and **the least precision of a rail is 128 bits** (the owner, 2026-11-02, after E4). (3) and (4) are not answered yet.

### E5 done: nothing is lost when a ship on a rail is stored (2026-11-02)

**The owner's rule (2026-11-02).** A path planned to the millimetre must lose nothing by saving, loading, freezing, thawing, parking or unparking; "if that means that 4096 bit fixed points need to saved into the world on long missions, then so be it". The same message: LGPL libraries are acceptable, and the repository will be GPL or AGPL (see the decisions of E4). Marked **[measured]**, **[read]** (from the code, not run) or **[mine]**.

**What a ship is, and what is written of it [read].** `MovingGrid` (`world_storage/src/moving_grids.rs`) holds: its level; its `pose` (a brick, an `i128` an axis, and an `f64` offset in the brick; the turn is four `f64`); the linear and the angular velocity (`f64`); its weight and centre of mass (**not saved**: the bodies work them out from the blocks, and a ship out of its file has none until they do); `held`; `frozen` (**not saved**: a ship out of a file is awake); what its pilot's logout does; its voxels; its terrain (a planet); its `rail`; and the sections of plugins that nobody here reads (kept byte for byte). A ship's file (`ship_file.rs`) holds the voxels, `engine:grid_flight` (the pose, both velocities as the bits of `f64`, held, logout) and `engine:rail`; the world file lists each ship with its pose and bounds only (`GridRecord`, `ParkedGrid`) and the clock of the world (`engine:clock`, a `u64` of ticks). **A rail is eight `f64` and the number of the body it goes round** (`encode_rail`: the normal, the axis, the period, the phase, the ratio and the periapsis, each the bits of an `f64`; a spin adds five), written and read back to the bit.
**Pose and velocity while a ship is on a rail [read].** `put_ships_on_their_rails` writes the ship's place (the rail's place for the centre of mass, less the centre of mass turned as the ship is), its velocity and its turn into the ship **every tick**: they are a copy of what the rail says, kept so that the rest of the game can read them. The file holds the copy too, and the first tick after a load puts it right. The **turn** of a coasting ship is not on the rail (a ship that spins does not coast): it is the quaternion of the pose, constant while the ship coasts.
**What `ExactOffset` is [read].** `{ bricks: [i128; 3], rest: [f64; 3] }` (`exact_circle.rs`): where a rail puts a body from its centre at a time, in whole bricks of 32 voxels and a rest below a brick that is a whole number of 2^-32 voxel. It is the **answer** of the 126-bit fixed-point solver, not something stored: it lives from the evaluation of a rail to the line that adds it to the centre's `WorldPosition` (`translated_by_bricks`), and nothing saves it. So the 2^-32 m of the E4 text, and the 2^-64 m proposed there, are the step of the *answer*. The precision in which a ship's path is *stored* today is the 53 bits of each of eight `f64`.

**Measured [`games/voxunami_cave/tests/orbit.rs`, the whole of Cave, Windows].** A ship on an ellipse round the planet (axis 74,540 voxels, ratio 0.939, a lap of 635 s), on its rail after a second, goes through (1) frozen for 100 ticks and let go, (2) put in its file for 100 ticks (`park_ship` with "waits for those aboard", so that nothing brings it back sooner) and brought back, (3) a world saved with `Server::save` and a server made from the save, (4) three rounds of all three, in order. In every case the rail, the pose and the velocity are **equal to those of an untouched ship of the same make at the same tick, bit for bit** (`assert_eq!` on `Rail`, `GridPose` and `[f64; 3]`), also in the tick the ship is brought back and the three after it (the centre of mass, which the file does not keep, is back by the end of that tick: there is no jump). Three mutations are caught: freezing a ship takes it off its rail (the freeze test and the combined one fail); the save writes the axis as an `f32` (the file, restart and bringing-back tests fail); a ship comes out of its file without its rail (file and restart fail). **The negative control:** the orbit made again from the place and the speed of the ship on it (`coasting_rail`, what every push does today) differs from the stored one in the last digits of every element (1.4e-15 of the axis, 8.3e-16 of the ratio, 2.0e-15 of the period, 4.9e-15 rad of the periapsis, 4.4e-15 rad of the phase) and the two paths part by **1.0e-9 voxel a lap** (9.6e-10 after one lap, 9.8e-7 after a thousand, 9.0e-4 after a million, which is 20 years of this orbit). That is nothing for this orbit and a lot for a thin one (E1: a part in 10^8 of the apoapsis at a ratio of 1.5e-9). E3, a spike on the codec alone (1,000 saves and loads), found the same: the elements are kept exactly.
**What does change a rail [read].** Only a making again from a state: (1) the five setters that `disturb` a ship (`set_grid_pose`, `apply_grid_force`, `apply_grid_torque`, `set_grid_held`, `set_grid_velocity`) take it off its rail; (2) `update_coasting` takes it off when it is held, turns, is in a level with a pull of its own, goes round nothing that is there, or something is near it (another grid, the carry sphere of a planet, a sphere of influence it is not inside); (3) `answer_a_change_of_mass` makes it again at an apsis when the planet's mass moved by more than a part in a billion; and the next rail is made by `Orbit::from_state` once the ship has been left alone for a second. **Freezing, thawing, parking, bringing in, saving and loading are not among them.**
**Two things I saw that are not precision [read, not run].** (a) The check of a ship that went away with its pilot, when it is brought in (`grid_lifecycle`, `read_parked_ship` and `Hull::is_free`), looks at the pose the file holds, which for a ship on a rail is where it *was*, not where the rail puts it now. (b) A parked ship is out of the world: nothing can hit it, and `update_coasting` does not look at it until it is brought in.

**What it says about the design [mine].**
1. The owner's rule holds for the rail we have, and the tests above keep it so. The rule that keeps it true: **nothing but a push or a collision may make a rail again from a state**; for a planned path the push is a commit (the lazy rails of E4), and a commit is the pilot's own act.
2. Wide numbers in the file are cheap. 4,096 bits is 512 bytes a number, and eight of them about 4 KB a ship; the container has varints and length-prefixed integers already. The cost of width is in evaluation, not in the file.
3. The cost of a tick need not grow with the stored width: a place to 2^-64 m needs the leading 200 to 256 bits of the period and the axis (the fraction of a turn from the clock and the wide period by a 256-bit division, a Kepler solve at 192 to 256 bits, the basis of the plane kept between commits); the other digits are for the assists to come and are used when a hand-over is worked out. **An estimate from the timings of malachite (25 us at 256 bits), not measured.**
4. What E4 called *storage precision* ("192 or 256 bits for a ship") is **a proposal, not what the game has**: the path of a ship is stored in 53-bit numbers today, and widening them (a new version of `engine:rail`, the type `Orbit`, and the code that reads its fields) is a step not yet taken.

### E6 done: the number layer, `engine/wide_float` (2026-11-02)

**What the owner asked (2026-11-02).** (1) Build the number layer on `malachite`. (2) "The lowest precision we ever go to should be 128 bits for rails." (3) A client may do heavy calculations when a player predicts their own orbit far ahead (dozens of assists): "this wouldn't be a cheating risk because the server would not accept results of calculations from the client. It would only be accepting the player's choice of actions with their ship." Marked **[measured]**, **[read]** (from the code) or **[mine]**.

**What it is [read, and tested below].** A new engine crate, `wide_float`, over `malachite-float` 0.14 (LGPL-3.0-only; no other crate names it, so a swap is one crate). Dependencies: `binary_codec` and `malachite`; nothing of the game, so a client uses it as the server does.
- `Precision`: a width in bits, **128 to 2^24**. `Precision::new` is a `Result`, `Precision::of` is for constants (a constant below 128 does not compile). `doubled` and `widened_by` stop at the most, so a loop that doubles ends. **The owner's floor of 128 bits is in the type**: nothing built on this layer can work in less.
- `WideFloat`: a finite real number with its precision. Never a NaN, an infinity or a negative zero. Every operation is the **correctly rounded** value of the exact result (nearest, ties to even): a fact of mathematics and not of the library, which is why it is the same on Windows and Linux and in any version that passes the tests. Nothing uses the machine's floating-point unit, so there is no `libm` here and no fast-math to guard against; `f64` is only at the edges (`from_f64` is exact, `to_f64` is the nearest double).
- **The precision of a result is that of the argument with more bits**; `rounded_to` sets a width (exact when it widens). So a computation is at the width of its inputs, and working at 256 bits on a number stored in 4,096 is `rounded_to` first, in the open. `==` compares value *and* width (the same to the bit); `compare` compares values; `Hash` agrees with `==`.
- `+ - *` and negation are operators; they panic only for a result beyond about 2^(+-1.07e9), which nothing the game does reaches. What can fail returns `Result<_, NumberError>`: `divide` (by zero), `sqrt`, `ln`, `acosh` (outside the domain), `atan2` (of the origin), `exp`, `sinh`, `cosh` and `times_power_of_two` (**an exponent out of range, or an underflow to zero, is an error and never a silent zero or an infinity**). Cannot fail: `sin`, `cos`, `sin_cos`, `atan`, `square`, `abs`, `pi`. Exact scaling by a power of two, `floor_to_i128`, `floor_log2`, `to_parts` and `from_parts` (sign, odd mantissa as bytes, exponent: the library's types are not in the interface).
- Text: `[-]0x<hex of the odd mantissa>p<decimal exponent>` (`-0x3p-1` is -1.5), exact, written by `Display` (`Debug` adds `@bits`) and read by `WideFloat::parse`; the reference vectors use it.
- **The saved form** (`write_to`, `read_from`): the precision (varint), a byte for zero / above / below, the exponent (zigzag varint) and the odd mantissa (length-prefixed little-endian bytes). **A number has one encoding** (the reader refuses an even mantissa, a zero byte on top, a mantissa wider than the precision, an out-of-range exponent or precision), a number read is the number written *with its width*, and a number of few bits is short whatever its width: 0.1 widened to 4,096 bits is 12 bytes, 600 is 6, zero is 3, and a full 4,096-bit number is 512 bytes and about 6. The bytes are pinned by a test, since they will be in worlds and replays.
- `test_support` (a cargo feature): strategies for numbers of every width, for tests in other crates.

**How it is tested [measured].**
1. **Conformance** (`tests/conformance.rs`, `tests/reference/functions.txt`, made by `tests/reference/generate.py`): 921 results of 15 operations (`+ - * /`, `sqrt`, `sin`, `cos`, `atan`, `atan2`, `sinh`, `cosh`, `exp`, `ln`, `acosh`, `pi`) at **128, 129, 192, 256, 512, 1,024 and 4,096 bits**, worked out by other code than the library's (exact integers and fractions; mpmath at 256 bits more than the width asked for, **each result worked out at two working precisions that must agree**), with nearly cancelling sums, numbers near 1 for `ln` and `acosh`, arguments to 2^100 for `sin` and `cos`, and short mantissas. All 921 equal, on Windows and on Linux (WSL Ubuntu), so the same bits on both. *One surprise worth keeping:* the first run disagreed at the `acosh` of 1 + 2^-1022 at 1,024 bits. The library was right: mpmath at 2,048 bits and more gave its digits, and at 256 spare bits mpmath had lost them to cancellation. The generator now checks itself that way, and the reference is no longer taken on trust.
2. **Properties against an exact rational** (`tests/rounding.rs`, 400 cases each, widths 128 to 4,096, `malachite-q` and a rounding written out in integers in `tests/common`): sum, difference, product and quotient are the exact result rounded once to the wider width, also for differences of nearly equal numbers and for arguments of different widths; `rounded_to`, scaling, the root of an exact square (exact), `to_f64` (the nearest of its neighbours), comparison and negation.
3. **Basics and codec** (`tests/basics.rs`, `tests/codec.rs`): the limits of `Precision`; every `i128` and every finite `f64` exact; the edges of `floor_to_i128`; the text form and its refusals; the exponent range; the bytes of six numbers; 14 refused encodings; a changed byte decodes to something that writes back as the changed bytes (**one encoding**); arbitrary bytes never panic; a lying precision allocates nothing.
4. **Two bugs of mine, one found by a test and one by reading the code again:** `floor_to_i128` refused `i128::MIN` (the guard was one too tight; the edges test found it); `from_parts` (and so `parse` and the reader of the saved form) made a zero of a number whose exponent was too small to be a number, because it passed "exact" to the underflow check, so a file with such an exponent would have been read as zero. Found in review, not by a test; it has regression tests now (`from_parts`, `parse` and a refused encoding); with the fault put back, two of them fail (checked).
5. **Mutations, five caught, each undone from a copy:** arithmetic rounding one bit short (the assertion and the vectors), subtraction done as addition (two property tests), the reader accepting an even mantissa (two tests), an underflow to zero not noticed (one), the writer forgetting the sign (three).
6. `cargo clippy --all-targets -D warnings`, `cargo fmt --check` and `cargo doc` with warnings denied are clean for the crate; 41 tests (13 + 10 + 6 + 9 + 3 doc tests, one of which is a compile-fail test of the 128-bit floor).

**Speed [measured, release build, this machine, one core; microseconds per operation].** A throwaway program over the crate (not kept):
| bits | add | multiply | divide | sqrt | sin_cos | atan2 | exp | a Kepler solve (e = 0.9, Newton at one width) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 128 | 0.04 | 0.07 | 0.15 | 0.17 | 1.6 | 8.4 | 10.8 | 16 |
| 256 | 0.06 | 0.11 | 0.22 | 0.22 | 3.5 | 21 | 15 | 33 |
| 1,024 | 0.05 | 0.27 | 0.44 | 0.44 | 12 | 64 | 45 | 130 |
| 4,096 | 0.08 | 1.4 | 5.0 | 2.2 | 97 | 540 | 295 | 1,310 |
So a place at 128 to 256 bits costs about **16 to 35 us** by a plain Newton solve (the 126-bit fixed-point rail we have takes 3.5 to 5.6 us); by these numbers [an estimate, not a measured rail] a hundred ships on wide rails would be 2 to 4 ms of a 50 ms tick, before the saving of starting the Newton steps narrow and ending wide. The cost of the width is in the functions and not in the arithmetic: an addition at 4,096 bits is 0.08 us.

**What I decided [mine, say if you disagree].**
1. The width travels with the number and a result is as wide as its widest argument. The other design (a context that says the width of every operation) is MPFR's; it needs no `max` rule but it makes every call say a width, and it lets a stored number of 4,096 bits drag a whole evaluation up to 4,096 unless someone remembers to round it.
2. `==` includes the width, because a value read from a file must be the value that was written, with the width it will be computed at: otherwise the same world could evaluate at different widths before and after a save, which is the loss the owner ruled out (E5).
3. The floor of 128 bits is a property of the type and not a convention. The price: the library cannot be tested here at 53 or 64 bits, where an exhaustive test is easy; the game never uses those, and the reference file starts at 128 (and 129, an odd width).
4. Errors and no NaN: `Result` for what can fail, operators for what cannot fail in practice.
5. **Not built yet: ball arithmetic and the doubling loop of E4.** They are written *on* this type and are needed when a decision of the server must be certain (which sphere is entered, whether an orbit is bound), which comes with hyperbolas and hand-overs.

**The client may work out previews (owner, 2026-11-02) [mine: what follows from it].**
- **The rule:** nothing a client computes is ever sent to the server as a result. The client sends the player's choices (a throttle, a burn, a button pressed); the server runs the same code from the same state and decides. A preview is display only, so a client whose arithmetic is wrong, or lies, misleads only the player who runs it. It adds no cheating risk to the rule the project already has (the client's view of the world comes only from the server's messages, and its commands are intentions, not results).
- **What it asks of the number layer, and what the layer gives:** the same bits on the client and on the server (it does: correct rounding), so a client's preview *is* what the server will do, which is also point (6) of the fine-tuning promise; no global state and `Send + Sync` types, so a preview runs in a thread of its own and never in the frame (a `WideFloat` owns its digits and shares nothing); an operation cannot be interrupted (one sine at 8 million bits takes seconds), so a long preview **checks a cancel flag between operations**, a flyby at a time, and keeps its results by (state, horizon) so that the button of E4 only adds a segment.
- **What it asks of the rest of the game [needs]:** the client can only start from numbers the server has sent, so **the exact state of a ship's path (the wide elements) must be in the message stream** in the saved form above, not rounded to `f64`; and the server's limits (the horizon it will work out for itself, the widest it will go) must be known to the client, so that a preview does not promise what the server will not do.

**Not done.** The wide `Orbit` and `engine:rail` (the next step), hyperbolas and the hand-over, balls and the doubling loop, burn programs, the message that carries the wide state.

### E7 done: Kepler's equation and the place of a body in wide numbers, `engine/wide_kepler` (2026-11-02)

**What it is for.** The step after the number layer: the evaluation that a rail with wide elements needs, built and tested **beside** the game, not in it. Nothing in the game calls it yet. Marked **[measured]**, **[read]** or **[mine]**.

**What it is [read].** A new engine crate, `wide_kepler` (depends on `wide_float` and `libm`). `Elements` are the elements `world_storage::rail::Orbit` has (the plane's `normal`, `radius` = the semi-major axis, `ratio` = 1 - e, `periapsis`, `period`, `phase` = the mean anomaly at time 0), each a `WideFloat`; `Time` is a count of units and how many make a second (the world's clock, under 2^96 units); `Elements::place_at(time, precision)` gives a `Place` (the position from the centre in voxels, the velocity in voxels a second), each component a number of `precision` bits (128 or more). The definition is the one of the game's rails and of `world_storage/tools/exact_ellipse_reference.py` (the plane's first direction is the +x axis taken into the plane, +z when the stored normal's x is 0.9 or more, by the number given and not by the unit vector, as `exact_circle::plane` always decided; the periapsis turns the place in the plane; the mean anomaly is the phase plus `time / period` taken modulo one **exactly**). The limits are the rails' (a ratio from 2^-64, a radius under 2^121 voxels, a period to 2^80 seconds, angles to 2^63 radians) and one new one: a period of at least 2^-20 seconds (the rails take any period above nothing).
**How [read].** Everything is done 96 bits wider than asked (64 because the equation's slope is as small as the ratio, down to 2^-64, and 32 for the roundings), the turns gone in 192 bits wider than that (the clock has 96 bits and a short period adds 20, and the fraction after them must still have its 64 + 8). Kepler's equation is solved by Newton's method on the half turn from a first guess in `f64` (the same guess the fixed-point solver uses, with `libm`, so the same on every machine), in the form `ratio E + e (E - sin E) - M`, whose terms are all of one sign where they could cancel (so there is no cancellation that matters, which I first thought needed extra bits for small E and does not: the absolute error of `E - sin E` is 2^-w of E, and the main term is `ratio E`, so the relative error of the residual is 2^-w over the ratio, which is the 64 bits already counted); the step is under 2^-(width - 70) of the answer to stop, so how many steps it takes is a property of the numbers and not of the clock or the machine, and it is bounded (64). A mean anomaly in the second half turn is the mirror image of one in the first.

**What the answer is worth [measured].** 146 rows (`tests/reference/orbits.txt`, made by `tests/reference/generate.py`: the definition in mpmath at 700 bits more than the width, from the exact elements written in the row), at 128, 192, 256, 512 and 1,024 bits: random elements and times; the rows where the equation is hardest (the thinnest ellipses, 2^-64, with the body at its periapsis, at an eccentric anomaly of 0, 2^-20 and 2^-136, and at the apoapsis, and an ellipse barely not a circle); and **a clock near 2^96 units with a short period and a phase that cancels the turns gone so that the mean anomaly is tiny** (the rows that make the guard of the turns necessary: without them the guard could be cut from 192 bits to 8 and every test passed). **A place is within the last bit of the width asked (radius x 2^-width) in every row, a speed within 2 bits of it.** `tests/invariants.rs` (physics that the formulas of the code do not share): the vis-viva equation, the angular momentum along the normal and its size, a body is where it was a period ago, the answer at 128 bits is the answer at 1,024 to 128 bits, and a circle is a circle (a few hundred random orbits each). **Against the rails the game has** (`world_storage/tests/rail_wide.rs`, 300 random orbits of doubles and 6 pinned ones, 126-bit fixed point against 256-bit wide): the positions differ by at most **1.2e-10 voxel on the 6 pinned orbits** (printed) **and by under 3e-10 on 300 random ones in a run where I tightened the bound to see** (the rail's own step is 2^-32 = 2.3e-10; the committed bound is 1e-6, because the rail's own error at the sharpest ellipses at their periapsis can be larger and a random seed must not make the test flaky), and the speeds by at most 1.5e-16 of the speed on the pinned ones; a negative control (the body one tick later is more than 1e-3 voxel away) shows the test can fail. So **a ship that is on a rail today would not move by anything the fixed point can see if it were put on a wide one.** Seven mutations caught, each undone from a copy: either guard cut (the second only after the rows above were added), a reflected anomaly not reflected, the periapsis left out, a speed with the wrong sign, the minor axis left out of the speed, and Newton stopped after one step. Windows and Linux (WSL).

**Speed [measured, release, one core].** A place and a speed: **30 us (a circle) to 44 us (an ellipse) at 128 bits, 60 at 256, 177 at 1,024, 1.3 ms at 4,096**, whatever the ratio (2^-64 costs the same as 0.3). The fixed-point rail takes 3.5 to 5.6 us. So this is about **8 times slower than the rail at 128 bits**: fine for a preview or a few ships (a hundred ships would be 4 ms of a 50 ms tick), not for a thousand, and not yet optimised: of the 44 us, `pi` is computed twice per call (3 and 4.5 us), the sine and cosine of the periapsis again (2.8) and the plane's directions (some 4), all of which are the same for every tick of an orbit [mine: a prepared form that keeps them would save about a third; not measured].

**What it says [mine].**
1. The wide rail is feasible with no loss against today's rails, and the guards are known: the cost of the width is in the functions, and the 128-bit floor is comfortable.
2. **The next step is the one with the ripple:** making `world_storage::rail::Orbit` hold wide elements and `engine:rail` a new version. `Orbit` is `Copy` and made of doubles and is named in 15 files (98 lines; 32 `.orbit` accesses): `coasting.rs`, `fit_orbit.rs` (making an orbit from a state, `Orbit::from_state`, which is double arithmetic), the ship and rail tests, Cave's orbit tests. Reading a version-2 rail would be exact (a double is exact in any width) and the E5 tests would be extended to the new form; **I did not start it**, because it changes a type that most of the rail code is written on, and it needs the owner's decisions listed at the end of E4 (lazy exact rails or a rounded commit, and the step of the answer).
3. `Orbit::from_state` (the making of an orbit from a place and a speed, which every push does) is the other half: in doubles it loses the digits a wide rail would keep (E5, E1), and a wide version is the work that makes a planned path survive a push.

### E8 done: the rails of the game are evaluated by `wide_kepler` (2026-11-03)

**What it is for.** The owner answered the three questions at the end of E7 (lazy exact rails; the 2^-64 m step; widen `Orbit`), and this is the first piece of the third: **where a body on a rail is, is now worked out by `wide_kepler`**, so the 126-bit fixed point of `exact_circle` and `exact_kepler` is gone. The type `Orbit` and `engine:rail` are not changed yet (the next step); this one changes only the evaluator under them, so that it could be verified alone. Marked **[measured]**, **[read]** or **[mine]**.

**What changed [read].** `Orbit::at` makes the exact elements of the doubles it holds (any width holds a double), asks `Elements::place_at` for the place and the speed in a width of at least 128 bits and wide enough that the last bit is below 2^-64 voxel (`answer_precision`: the bits of the radius, one for the far end of an ellipse, and 64, to the next multiple of 64: 128 bits up to a radius of 2^63, 192 at the largest), and rounds **once**: the place to whole bricks and a rest in steps of 2^-32 voxel, half a step up (`ExactOffset::of_voxels`, new, `exact_offset.rs`), the speed to the nearest `f64`. `ExactOffset` is **the same type**, so nothing that places a body in the world changed. Removed: the CORDIC sine and cosine, `circle_point`, the whole Kepler solver (`exact_kepler.rs`), the signed, dividing and root-taking half of `Wide`, and the tests of all of them (the code and its tests are 3,692 lines out and 472 in); what stays of the fixed point is what `Orbit::from_state` and `Orbit::apsides_passed` still use (the directions of the plane and the angle as a fraction of a turn in 128 bits), until they are widened. `equation_in_f64` was in both `wide_kepler` and `fit_orbit`: it is now `wide_kepler::equation_in_f64` and `fit_orbit` uses that.
**Two limits that are new [read].** An orbit has a **period of at least 2^-20 second** (a little under a microsecond), either way: `wide_kepler` asks for it (the part of a turn gone is worked out in at most 116 whole bits), `Orbit::elliptical` and `decode_rail` refuse less (a spin has no such bound; no body file or test makes an orbit that fast). And a **`RailTime` is under 2^96 units** either way (`From<f64>` takes a time further than 2^76 seconds as exactly that far; a clock of ticks never reaches it).

**What the old rail was worth, measured before it was removed [measured].** A sweep of 6,000 random orbits (radius 2^-10 to 2^120, ratios to 2^-64, periods 0.05 s to 2^80 s, any tick up to `u64::MAX`, four clock rates), the fixed-point rail against `wide_kepler` at 320 bits: **up to a radius of 2^80 the old answer was the true one rounded to the step** (1.16e-10 voxel = half a step at most, in every sample); at 2^80 to 2^90 it was 1.25 steps out, at 2^90 to 2^100 **2.2e-7 voxel**, at 2^100 to 2^110 **2.7e-4 voxel**, and at 2^110 to 2^120 **0.27 voxel** (the fixed point ran out of bits; the docs said "a small fraction of a voxel", and the pinned tests did not look at radii past 2^80). The speeds differed by at most 5.6e-16 of the speed.

**What the new one is worth [measured].** Three tables of bits were pinned from the old code: the 26 circles (`rail_circle_bits.rs`), the 15 ellipses (`rail_ellipse.rs`) and the 6 orbits made from a state (`rail_fit.rs`). Some of every table moved, and **I did not trust the new bits because the code gave them**: the circles' new values were worked out again by mpmath at 300 digits and the ellipses' at 900 bits (the definition of `wide_kepler`'s `generate.py`), from the elements as the program keeps them (the normal as an `f64` unit vector), and **all 41 agree to the bit** (the position rounded to the step, the speed the nearest `f64`; my first check of 3 circles disagreed because it normalised the raw normal exactly and not the `f64` one the rail holds, which is how the tolerance of a rail is defined: the checker was wrong, not the rail). Everything else did not move, including **every worked-out pin of `rail.rs`** (120 digits, a step of 2^-31 allowed), the 4 apsis tests and the file tests: **the workspace has 2,705 tests and none fails** (2,726 before, the difference being the removed tests of the old solver less the new ones). `rail_wide.rs` was the bridge from the old rail and is now a test that a rail's answer is the wide answer rounded once: the place within half a step (a hair over) and the speed within half an ulp, 300 random orbits to a radius of 2^120 and ratios down to 1e-19, and the pinned orbits, with the same negative control. 3 new tests of `rail.rs` (the period bound, the shortest orbit is where it began after any whole number of its periods up to the last tick, a time beyond the clock is that far and never a panic), 5 of `exact_offset.rs` (the rounding, the carry into the next brick, the largest place, and two properties against exact integers). Seven mutations, each caught and undone from a copy: the place always in 128 bits (3 tests), the rest rounded down (3), the carry into the next brick left out (2), an orbit or a decoded orbit with any period (1), a time not held to the clock (1), the bricks rounded toward nothing (4).

**What it costs [measured, release, one core].** `Rail::state` for an orbit: **32 us for a circle, 46 us for an ellipse** (the same at a radius of 7e6 and of 1e18, and with a ratio of 1e-12), **54 us in the 192 bits of a radius of 1e30**. The old rail took 3.5 to 5.6 us (E7, not measured again on this build, since the old code is gone), so a rail is **8 to 10 times slower**; `apply_rails` runs **twice** a tick (at the start, and after the coasting update, which "puts again what was put"), so each ship on a rail costs two states: ten rails are about a millisecond of a tick of fifty, a hundred would be 10 ms, which is not acceptable for a game with a hundred ships on rails. Nothing does that yet [read]. **[mine]** Two savings that are known and not made: the second `apply_rails` could skip the rails it has just evaluated (about half), and pi is worked out twice per call (3 and 4.5 us), and the sine and cosine of the periapsis and the directions of the plane (some 7 us together) are the same for every tick of an orbit, so a prepared form kept with the orbit would take about a third off; I did not make either because it is a cache on a type that is about to change, and nothing is slow yet.

**The 2^-64 m step [mine, owner's yes].** The owner agreed to a finer step of the answer. `ExactOffset` keeps **2^-32** because the world cannot hold more: it is added to a `WorldPosition`, whose offset in its brick is an `f64` (an ulp of 2^-48 in a brick of 32), and a rest of 2^-64 would be rounded away on the next line. The finer answer is the **wide place itself**, `Elements::place_at(time, precision)`, to any width (the last bit below 2^-64 voxel is what `answer_precision` already gives); that is what a hand-over between rails, a preview, or a chain of assists will use, and `ExactOffset::of_voxels` is the only place that rounds. If the owner wants the rail's own answer finer, `ExactOffset` and the `translated_by_bricks` it feeds change together; I have not done it.

**What it says [mine].**
1. The evaluator is the right one to build on: it is the definition, checked against mpmath, and it **fixes a real error** (up to 0.27 voxel at the largest radii) that the old tests did not look at because their pins were taken from the old code.
2. The pins in `rail_circle_bits.rs` said "if one moves, find out why before changing the number". I found out, for every one that moved, and the file says so; but **a pin taken from the code protects against change, not against error**, and these pins held that error (the position of pin 17 was 0.055 voxel out). The new ones are the truth to the bit, so they protect against both.
3. The next step is the type: `Orbit` holds `Elements` (wide), `Rail` is no longer `Copy`, `engine:rail` goes to version 3 (version 2 is not read: no upgrade, as for version 1), the apsides are worked out from the wide mean anomaly, and the fit from a state (`Orbit::from_state`) is widened last.

### E10 done: the fit from a state is in wide numbers, and nothing is snapped to a circle (2026-11-03)

**The owner's decisions of 2026-11-03, which this step carries out or records.** (1) **Do not snap to a circle at all**: an orbit with an eccentricity below 1e-14 may be shown as a circle in the map interface someday, but is never simplified in a rail or in a file; it may be brought back if there is a good enough reason. (2) A **server setting for how many sphere-of-influence changes** a path may have, default **100**. (3) A **server setting for the widest number a rail may have**, default **65,536 bits**. (4) The licence **starts as AGPL** (GPL may be offered later). Marked **[measured]**, **[read]** or **[mine]**. Of the four, only (1) is built here; (2) and (3) are settings for code that does not exist yet (the hand-over between rails, and wide numbers beyond the 128 to 232 bits the game makes today), and **[mine]** a setting that nothing obeys is a lie in the configuration, so they are to be built with the first code that obeys them, next to the horizon (E4: the number of assists and the width they need are one question); (4) is recorded in `decisions/world-and-sky.md`.

**What it is [read].** `wide_kepler::Elements::from_state(position, velocity, mu, time, precision)` is the inverse of `place_at`: the orbit through a place and a speed, from numbers of any width, in elements of `precision` bits. `world_storage::rail::Orbit::from_state` (an exact offset of bricks and a rest, and a speed of doubles), `from_place` (doubles) and the new `from_wide_state` (any width, and the width of the elements asked for) call it and go through `Orbit::from_elements`. The working is 128 bits wider than the elements (the energy `2 / r - v^2 / mu` loses as many bits as the orbit is long over its periapsis, up to 65, the angular momentum as many as the state is nearly along the radius, and they multiply into the ratio: 65 + 32 + 32 are under 128); the plane is made from the normal **as it will be kept** (rounded to the width), because the axis the circle starts from is decided by that number's x; the eccentric anomaly comes from the true anomaly as two parts of an arctangent; the mean anomaly as `ratio E + e (E - sin E)`; the period from Kepler's third law, kept at its width before the turns gone since time 0 are worked out with it, exactly. **No rule decides that an orbit is near enough something else**: the thresholds that were noise tolerances of an `f64` state (a circle below an eccentricity of 1e-14, no plane below 1e-14 of the place times the speed, near escape within 1e-13) are gone. A state that is exactly a circle is a circle (its eccentricity is nothing: the eccentricity vector is exactly nothing), one a hair off is an ellipse with the hair; what a state cannot be is refused (on the centre, at rest or falling straight, at escape speed or more, a mass that is not above nothing), and what an orbit cannot be is refused as an orbit by the limits (a ratio under 2^-64, a radius over 2^120, a period over 2^80 seconds). Removed with the `f64` fit: `exact_circle.rs` (the plane, the angle as a fraction of a turn, the 128-bit products), `wide.rs` and their tests, and the parts of `tools/exact_circle_reference.py` that made their tables; `equation_in_f64` is private to `wide_kepler` again.

**What the phase is, and what I found [measured].** The first run reproduced every state to 4 bits except **three rows, one in each of three widths, which lost 28 to 32 bits of the place and 91 to 96 of the speed**: the body exactly at the periapsis of the thinnest ellipse (a ratio of 2^-64). Its mean anomaly is a tiny number below nothing, and an angle from 0 to 2 pi stores that as 2 pi less a tiny number, rounded to the width of the element. The cause is not the code but the orbit: near the nearest point of a thin ellipse a change of the mean anomaly moves the body by the ratio to the power of a half (2^32 times) and its speed by the ratio to the power of one and a half (2^96 times, which is exactly what was lost). So **the phase is an angle from -pi to pi and has more bits than the other elements, `ceil(1.5 x log2(1 / ratio)) + 8` more** (8 for an ordinary orbit, 104 for the thinnest); every other element has the width asked for. Then the state is reproduced to **2 bits (the place) and 1 bit (the speed)** in all 52 rows. This is also a limit that no width removes: a stored period or phase of `p` bits reproduces the state at the time of the fit, and drifts by `2 pi x turns x 2^-p` after that (E4's bits per assist).

**What it is worth [measured].** `wide_kepler/tests/fit.rs` (8 tests) against `reference/fit.txt`, **52 rows made by `reference/generate_fit.py` (a second program: elements chosen, the place and the speed worked out forward in mpmath, the state rounded to 256 bits more than the width)**, at 128, 192, 256 and 512 bits, with thin ellipses (2^-64 to 2^-19), orbits barely not a circle (e = 2^-(width / 2)), and bodies at the apsides: **the radius, the ratio, the period and the normal found equal the rows' to the last bit in every row**, the angles are within a bit (compared only where the state determines them: the angle of the nearest point of an orbit that is a circle to 2^-100 is not something a state can say, its error being 2^-(width + 128) over the eccentricity, 126 bits at e = 2^-256), the place and the speed come back from `place_at` within 2 and 1 bits, and the phase is within (-pi, pi]. Also: an exact circle is exactly a circle (ratio 1, periapsis 0, phase 0, radius 4); **a speed 2^-60 under escape is the ellipse the energy says** (a = 2^58, an energy that is the difference of two numbers that agree to 58 bits, found within 2 bits); every state that is no ellipse is refused; the same exact state given in 128, 192 or 1,024 bits is the same orbit; **a normal that rounds across the 0.9 that decides the axis still gives the orbit back** (the case a random row would not find); and a negative control (the state is that of its time only). In `world_storage`, `rail_fit.rs` (12 tests): five of the old ten had to change because they pinned the noise rules: the circle is now "as near a circle as its state says" (an eccentricity under 1e-13, and **not** exactly a circle, asserted), the 1e-15 case is the ellipse it is, a hair under escape is the huge ellipse (1.2e13 times the distance, a period of 1e21 seconds) and not refused, `from_place` equals `from_state` for an offset a double holds, and the 6 pins of the f64 bits of the elements became **6 hashes of the bytes of the whole orbit** (which say that nothing changed and not that it is right; the rows say that); the two property tests and the fixed cases of the orbit it came from (the bounds are those of the `f64` fit) pass **unchanged**; and **new: the orbit found puts the body in the very same offset, bricks and rest, at all six radii up to 8.8e26 voxels** (a double of a place there is 1.9e11 voxels from the next, and the test fails if the offset is taken as an `f64`). Mutations (13, 12 caught), caught: the guard cut to 8 and to 64 bits (and 80 fails by hand: 128 is the least of the sizes I tried that passes), the phase without its extra bits, the phase from 0 to 2 pi (caught only after I added the assertion on its range, which the extra bits had hidden), the plane made from the normal before it is rounded, the period not kept at its width, the periapsis from the wrong end, the offset taken as an `f64`, the wrong clock rate; a mutation of the ratio's form was **not** caught because the form was redundant (the guard bits make `1 - e` as good), and I removed the form.

**What it costs [measured, release, one core].** One fit is **114 us for an ordinary ellipse, 145 us for a state near a circle** (the old `f64` fit took a few microseconds). `update_coasting` fits every ship that is not on a rail, every tick, to see whether it may go on one (`coasting_rail`): a hundred free ships are **11 to 14 ms of a tick of 50**, on top of the rails. Nothing does that yet [read]. Savings known and not made at the time (the first two were made in E11): the cheap test of whether anything is near could come before the fit; a ship still inside the second it must be quiet for need not be fitted every tick; pi is worked out twice per fit and the plane of the orbit again at every place.

**A judgement call [mine], to be checked.** A ship on a rail answers a change of the mass of its planet at the nearer or the farther end of its ellipse, or at once if the orbit is a circle. A ship fitted from a state is now never exactly a circle, so by that rule it would wait up to a whole lap. `coasting.rs` has a constant, `NEARLY_A_CIRCLE` = 1e-14 (the owner's figure for the map), under which it answers at once, because the two ends of such an ellipse are within 1e-14 of each other and there is nothing to choose between them. It decides **when** the ship is put on the orbit its state is on; it changes no orbit and nothing in a file. If the owner wants the ship to wait for an end whatever the eccentricity, it is the one constant (and one test, `a_circle_has_no_ends_and_is_answered_at_once`, says what it does).

**How it was run [measured].** rustfmt, clippy and rustdoc clean; the whole workspace gave **2,730 tests passed and one failed**: `games/voxunami_cave/tests/screen.rs`, `a_replay_draws_the_form_exactly_as_it_was_drawn_live...` (a GPU test that compares a replayed picture with the live one, on every adapter). It passed in all eight reruns (alone three times, its file twice, the whole Cave package of 550 tests once, and two more with the file), so I take it for a failure under load (other builds were running on the machine) and **did not find its cause**; it draws a form and does not touch a rail. Linux (WSL, kernel 6.18): `wide_float`, `wide_kepler`, `world_storage` and `server` give **439 tests passed, none failed**, with the hashes of the fitted orbits and the 52 rows.

### E11 done: the two savings in the cost of a tick (2026-11-03)

**What was asked.** The owner asked for the two savings that E10 named: a ship that cannot be put on a rail yet need not be fitted every tick, and a tick need not work out a rail twice. Marked **[measured]**, **[read]** or **[mine]**.

**What changed [read].**
1. **A ship is fitted when its second is up, not every tick.** `update_coasting` asks, every tick and of every ship that is not on a rail, only the rules that need no orbit (`may_be_put_on_a_rail`: a free ship that turns nothing, in a level with no pull of its own, with a planet that pulls it, and nothing near it). Only when the ship has passed them for the whole `QUIET_SECONDS`, undisturbed, is the orbit made from its state (`coasting_rail`, the costly part) to see whether the ship may have it; if not, the second begins again. So a ship that is pushed every tick is **never** fitted, one that is left alone is fitted **once**, and one whose orbit is refused is fitted once every 21 ticks. `coasting_rail` keeps its order of refusals for whoever asks it (its tests are unchanged). **The cost of the change [mine]:** the orbit-dependent rules (an orbit at all, not too low, inside the sphere of influence) are looked at at the end of the second and not at each of its ticks. A ship that is quiet but whose orbit is refused, and is allowed at the end of a second, goes on a rail then, though it was allowed for less than that. Pushes, moves and holds still restart the second at once, and so does anything near it, every tick.
2. **A rail is worked out once a tick.** `world_storage::rail::RailMemo` keeps, for each ship, the orbit and the time its answer was worked out for, and gives the answer back only for the very same orbit (every element, width and all) at the very same time. ### E14 done: hyperbolas in `wide_kepler`: the place and the speed, and the fit from a state (2026-11-03)

**What was asked.** "Continue" after E13, which is the next step of the order agreed: hyperbolic elements, evaluation and fit, tested on their own against mpmath; and a look at the growth of the second `apply_rails` (in "What is left" of E13). Marked **[measured]**, **[read]**, **[mine]**.

**The conventions [mine, for the owner to veto].** A hyperbola is an `Elements` with a **`ratio` below nothing** (`ratio = 1 - e`, so e is above 1, from 2^-64 to 2^64 either way); `radius` is the size of the semi-major axis `|a|`; `period` is 2 pi over the mean motion (negative for the other way round); **`phase` is the mean anomaly at time 0 as it is**, not taken modulo a turn, at most 2^120 either way (an ellipse's stays at 2^63 and a part of a turn). I chose this over storing the time of periapsis that E9 foresaw: it is the same number (minus the mean motion times the time), exact in the same way, and needs no new field, so `Elements`, `PreparedElements` and the formulas have one shape. A parabola (`ratio` 0) is refused. `mean_anomaly_in_turns` refuses a hyperbola (it goes round no turn). **It is not a rail yet**: `Orbit::from_elements` refuses a hyperbola, because its views, apsides, bytes and the server's rules are an ellipse's; that one gate says no until each is made for one.

**What was built [read].**
1. `Elements::validate` knows both; `Elements::prepare` and `PreparedElements::place_at` have a `Motion` (ellipse or hyperbola) and the same plane and periapsis turning for both. The ellipse's arithmetic moved verbatim: **the 240 places pinned in E12 are the same to the bit**, and so are the E10 fit pins in `world_storage`.
2. The equation `M = e sinh H - H` (`kepler.rs`): the first guess in `f64` kept in a bracket (the function is increasing and convex); Newton's method in wide numbers held under a bound above the root (`ln(4 M) + 3`, and 3 for the small ones), written as `(e - 1) sinh H + (sinh H - H)`, whose terms are not below nothing; the place from `x = |ratio| - 2 sinh^2(H/2)` and `r = |ratio| cosh H + 2 sinh^2(H/2)` (units of `|a|`) so that nothing cancels where it need not.
3. **`sinh_and_cosh`** from one exponential where the argument is not small (malachite's `exp`, `sinh` and `cosh` cost 12 to 14 us each at 224 bits and a sine and cosine 2.5): a place far out went from 127 us to **68**; near the periapsis, where the two functions are still used, it is 105 to 126 us.
4. **The fit** (`Elements::from_state`, `fit.rs`): the energy `2/r - v^2/mu` below nothing is a hyperbola (exactly nothing a parabola, refused); the periapsis, the plane and the shape as for an ellipse; **the anomaly from the distance where the body is not near its periapsis** (`cosh H = (r/|a| + 1)/e`, a sum of positive numbers, and the radial speed gives the sign) **and from the true anomaly where it is** (Newton for `v sinh h - u cosh h`, `tanh h = u / v`, `h = H/2`, from `asinh` in `f64`); the period from the mean motion; the phase as the mean anomaly less the mean motion times the time, worked out with 192 bits more than the width and **kept to the bits of its size and of what the thinness of the orbit magnifies it by**. A fit costs 13 us near the periapsis and 91 us far out [measured, release, 128 bits].

**What was found [measured].**
- **Far out the true anomaly says almost nothing about H.** The first version took H from the true anomaly everywhere (`tanh(H/2) = sqrt((e-1)/(e+1)) tan(nu/2)`), and a body with a phase of 2^86 did not settle: near the asymptote the direction is magnified by e^H. The distance has no such problem; near the periapsis the distance form cancels (`cosh H - 1`) and the true anomaly is the good one. They meet at `cosh H` of 2.
- **The phase needs its whole bits.** It is not reduced, so a body 2^76 seconds after time 0 has a phase of 99 bits, and stored in the width alone the body would not be where it was. The phase is kept to `width + its whole bits + 1.5 x the bits the ratio is under 1 + 8` (the ellipse's rule, plus the whole bits).
- **The angular momentum is not a good check far out**: for a hyperbola that is barely one the body goes almost straight away, `r x v` is a small difference of large products, and it loses 38 bits that are the check's and not the code's; it is compared in units of `(r v)^2`.
- A state from `place_at` at only the width gives a thin hyperbola's ratio to 2^-190 (its energy cancels 61 bits): the state is given 256 bits more than the fit is asked for, as in the ellipse's rows.

**How it was checked [measured].** Tests first. `reference/generate_hyperbola.py` makes 206 rows in mpmath **by a way the Rust does not take** (the true anomaly, the distance from the conic's equation and the speed from the angular momentum, where the Rust goes by the hyperbolic functions of H) at 700 bits more than the width, and **checks itself** (the other way gives the same place and speed to 200 bits under the working precision, or it stops): widths 128 to 1,024, the thinnest hyperbola with the body at the periapsis, a mean anomaly of 2^-136 to 2^90 and of a tenth to twenty on the thinnest, the other way round, and a clock of 2^96 units with a phase that cancels it. **The place and the speed lose at most -1 bit: the answer is right to its last bit in every row.** Without a reference: the speed at each distance (energy) and the angular momentum to 253 of 256 bits (the test asks for 232), the mirror image about the periapsis and the other way round being the same path run backwards (to the bit), the phase not taken modulo a turn, a prepared hyperbola giving `place_at`'s place, the limits refused. `reference/generate_fit_hyperbola.py` makes 76 rows (elements chosen, the state worked out forward): **the radius, the ratio and the period are found to the last bit, the normal to 2^-4 and the periapsis to 2^0 of the last bit, the phase with 28 bits to spare over what it needs; the body is put back with a loss of 1 bit of place and 1 of speed**, and a state taken a thousand seconds on gives the same hyperbola to 2^-123. The cases of the speed of escape: exactly it is a parabola and refused; 2^-40 over it is the hyperbola the energy says (radius 2^39, ratio -2^-38, to within the 2 bits allowed); 2^-100 over it is refused for its limits (the ratio would be 2^-98). The old E10 test that said "a hyperbola is on no ellipse" now says what is still true (a parabola, a straight flight, a mass of nothing). **19 new tests** (8 + 7 in `wide_kepler/tests`, 3 of the solver inside, 1 of the gate in `world_storage`). Mutations, each caught and undone from a copy: **26** (the evaluator: the side, the sign of the half versine, the distance, the phase, the guard bits, the bound above the root, the sign of the mean anomaly, cosh H, the minor axis, the limits, the turns, what is a hyperbola, the sign of the period; the fit: the sign of the energy, the route by the distance used never and always, the direction, the phase's guard bits and its own bits, the sign of M, a parabola, the mean motion, the sine and cosine, the sign of the clock). **Three survived at first and each made a test**: the bound above the root and the clamp under it (nothing reached them: the first guess is too good, so the bound has a test of its own that it is above the root of every hyperbola, and the clamp one that a guess a long way off is brought to the root), and the guard bits of the phase (my comparison was in units of the phase's own size, which is 2^99 for a body at its periapsis a long time on, so it passed any error; it is now in units of the mean anomaly at the time of the state).

## C. Long burns and previews

**What is known [source].** KSP (stock) does not thrust during on-rails time warp; players use mods that fake it (the forum threads say that thrust is ignored on rails and that "physics warp" is limited to a low factor); KSP 2 reportedly lets a ship accelerate under warp. Principia (the KSP mod) has a flight planner
that integrates burns; its library lists adaptive Runge-Kutta-Nystrom methods (Dormand-El-Mikkawy-Prince 4(3), Fine 5(4)) and many symplectic ones (Blanes-Moan, McLachlan), and its default ephemeris settings use a fixed-step Blanes-Moan 14A **[source: its `methods.hpp` and a forum post]**; I did not confirm which it uses for burns.
For low thrust over many orbits the literature averages the equations of the orbital elements over each orbit (Gauss's variational equations; equinoctial elements are the usual choice), with analytic results for constant tangential thrust **[source: arXiv 2204.06959 and the papers beside it]**. An ideal solar sail has an acceleration of beta mu / r^2 cos^2(alpha) along the sail's normal,
alpha the angle between the normal and the sun line, so the trajectory is the integral of that with a control law for alpha **[source: the sail papers returned by the search]**.

**The owner's idea, and why it works [mine].** A thrust program (when, how hard, which way) and a starting state **determine** the trajectory: it is a function, not something that has to be stepped in real time. So:
1. A **burn program** is data: start tick, end tick, thrust (or acceleration) as a piecewise polynomial in time, mass flow, and an attitude law (fixed in the inertial frame, fixed in the frame of the orbit, to the sun, a cone angle for a sail). For an ion drive, mass is m0 minus flow x time, exactly; a sail's thrust depends on the position (1/r^2), which is in the integrand.
2. A **deterministic integrator** (a fixed method, a step that is a pure function of the state and the program, `f64` with `libm`, no wall clock, no `std` transcendental functions; `clippy.toml` already bans them, `powi` too) turns the program into **segments**: state at the ends, and a polynomial (dense output) between, so the place and speed at any tick are one evaluation.
3. The **preview** is the same call on the client or server: the same code, the same inputs, the same bits (a rule the project already holds for terrain and for physics). The **time warp** reads the same segments. A ship that burns at 1x also reads them, so a warped burn and an unwarped one are the same trajectory, which a stepped Rapier burn could not promise.
4. Saved: the program and the starting state, not the segments (they are recomputed; saves hold ids and numbers, as the hard rules say).
5. A change of mind (the player changes the throttle) starts a new program from the state at that tick. A burn that is steered by the keys, tick by tick, is a program of one tick, made as it goes.
6. Gravity during a burn: the parent's pull only (patched conics), plus the thrust. Then the comparison with the exact rail is available as a check: with the thrust at zero the integrator must give back the rail within its own error, which is test C1.

**Integration choices [mine, from the sources].**
- *Cowell* (integrate the whole acceleration): simple; error grows along a long burn.
- *Encke* (integrate the difference from the rail, and make the rail again from the state when the difference is large): the rail part is exact and the integrator only carries the small part; it uses the exact Kepler code of section A for every stage. Probably the best fit here.
- *Averaged elements* (Gauss's equations averaged over an orbit): steps of many orbits, for spirals of thousands of turns; not exact, and its result differs a little from a stepped burn, so if it is used it must be used for **both** the preview and the warp, and then the 1x burn must read the same segments too (point 3). Held in reserve for the very long ones.
- *Step size* from the orbit (a fraction of the local period, so a close pass takes small steps) by an integer rule; cost for a year at a low orbit with 1/200 of a period steps is in the millions of steps: seconds, so a preview is computed in the background, cancellable, and cached by (program, state).

**Open points.** The integrator's order and step rule (E1 and C1 decide); how a program is entered in the UI (not this document); what a sail does when it meets a shadow (the planet's, cheaply, by a cylinder test at stage points) or the heating of its sail; time warp as a game clock that runs faster than ticks (`future-goals.md` already lists it); how many programs a server keeps for ships nobody watches.

## Questions and the owner's answers (2026-11-01)

1. **Gravity model on rails.** Patched conics: a ship feels only its parent's pull, and observed free flight is the same model, a kinematic body on its rail, so that observed and unobserved flight agree. **Yes.** The n-body pull of everything massive, which the game uses now, is then used only inside a shared frame for contacts (B5).
2. **Period.** For a ship on a rail it is **computed** from the parent's mass parameter and the semi-major axis. For planets in data packs **either is accepted**; when both are given and they differ, the pack's explicit values are used and **an error is logged**.
   *What "differ" means:* pack authors write round numbers (a period of 3600 s, a mass of 1e22 kg), which I expect are rarely consistent to better than a few parts in a thousand, so a tight tolerance would log an error for most hand-written planets. **Accepted by the owner (2026-11-01): relative 1e-3 on the period.**
3. **Hyperbolas** are the second step, after the ellipse.
4. **Epoch.** Not needed: see A (the phase at tick 0, worked out exactly when the orbit is made). Revisit for hyperbolas.
5. **Order of work.** A, then E1 to E3, then the ships' conversions (B5, B6), then C. The owner asked the rail-precision questions first: A2 answers them; **confirmed (2026-11-01): stay at 126 bits, and store `ratio` = 1 - e** (point 3 of A2). A0 and A1 are done; A2 (the solver) is next.
